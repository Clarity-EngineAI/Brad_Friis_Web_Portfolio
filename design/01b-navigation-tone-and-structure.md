**Brad Friis personal site and thought-leadership blog**

This document replaces the first Stage 1b entirely. That version was rejected. It is not preserved

## 1. Two answers that reset the project

Before any structure, two things changed on 11 August 2026 and both are larger than a navigation bar.

### 1.1 The references were requested. The central claim is dead.

Brad confirmed he asked Dr Andrew Bergh and Adrian Pilgrim for their references. Only Pranesh Lad's
email arrived unprompted.

Everything built on *nobody asked for these* now goes:

- the hero line in use, **"Three customers wrote references I never asked for."**
- `Unsolicited` as a page headline, which was already rejected on other grounds and is now also untrue
- the support paragraphs in hero options C, H and I, all of which carry the claim
- the navigation gloss "Three letters. Nobody asked for any of them."
- the `3 references — Written, signed, unsolicited` proof-strip line
- the framing of `/proof` in §4 of the Stage 1 brief, which ranks the Rototuna note as "the most
  obviously unsolicited" and implies the other two are unsolicited as well

This matters beyond copy. The claim would have appeared in public above three named people's
signatures, and two of those people could have contradicted it. That is a reputational exposure, not
a stylistic one, and the site is better for losing it now than after launch.

### 1.2 Five letters, not three, and they span three industries

> **Superseded in part, 11 August 2026, by `01d-reference-archive-audit.md`.** The scans arrived the
> same day and the archive turned out to be larger and messier than this section assumed. **The set is
> six letters across four industries — advertising, tourism, radio and education software — spanning
> 2003 to 2025.** Two documents are recommended for withholding and one sits below the six with its
> relationship disclosed. Every count in this document reading *five* should be read as *six*, and
> every *three industries* as *four*. The reasoning below is otherwise unchanged and still governs:
> the argument is span, and span is what the evidence supports.

Brad has two further references to add: one from an advertising agency in the earlier career, one
from Hawke's Bay Tourism. Both are unscanned as of 11 August 2026 and are carried through this
document as placeholders.

**This is a better asset than the one it replaces.** The old argument was about *how* the letters
arrived. The new one is about *span*:

> Five people. Three industries. Twenty-five years. All of it in writing.

Every word of that is true and checkable. It also closes the one hole a sceptical reader could pick
in the old evidence set — that all three letters came from education software, so the pattern might
be sector-specific rather than personal. Advertising in the 2000s, tourism, and education software in
the 2020s is a pattern nobody can explain away as a friendly sector.

The unsolicited idea is not wasted. It shrinks to one true sentence attached to one document: nobody
asked Pranesh Lad for his email. On the Lad letter, and nowhere else.

**Placeholder handling until the scans exist.** The letters page is designed for five and built with
five slots. Two carry a dated "to come" state rather than being hidden, because a page that grows
from three to five is a page that changes shape at launch. Neither placeholder goes live with a name
attached until its scan and its consent both exist.

**Consent.** Bergh, Pilgrim and Lad have all agreed to be named with their letters in full. That
closes decision 5 in the Stage 1 brief and removes it from the blocking list. **The two new
referees have not been asked.** They need the same written agreement before their names appear, and
contact details are withheld for all five regardless — Bergh's email and phone, and Lad's work email
and the internal ticket address in his signature, which the Stage 1 brief did not catch.

## 2. What the rejected attempt got right, and the one thing it got backwards

### 2.1 Right, and kept

**"Proof" fails.** Defensive, abstract, and the noun form of a banned phrase. That diagnosis stands
and the word stays dead.

**`/career` and `/about` are one page.** A first-person narrative career page *is* an about page.
Keeping both guarantees one is thin, and the thin one is `/about`, because everything with tension in
it lives in the career story. Community and governance fold in as the closing movement. Kept.

**Memory is encoded by contrast, not volume.** Five quirky labels is a theme, and a theme is a
costume. One unexpected item in an otherwise sober row is what a reader repeats to a colleague. This
is the single most useful rule the last attempt produced and every system below is judged against it.

**Evidence sits left of biography.** The letters are the differentiator; the career narrative is the
commodity. The nav order should reflect that.

### 2.2 Backwards, and reversed

The last attempt argued that a `Consulting` item makes a recruiter ask whether Brad wants a job or
has a business, and therefore the consultancy should be served sideways by a `/now` page.

**That reasoning is inverted.** The doubt a recruiter forms is not caused by disclosure. It is caused
by ambiguity. Clarity Engine AI is already the first entry on the master CV, already attached to the
Pikle work, and already on LinkedIn. A recruiter will find it. The question is only whether they find
it where Brad put it or somewhere he did not. Finding it later, after a site that read as pure
job-seeker, is what produces the thought *what else is he not saying* — and that is a worse thought
than *he consults, and he is looking for a permanent seat.*

A plainly stated consultancy destination that answers the objection in its own first paragraph costs
less than a concealed one. Brad chose this fork on 11 August 2026: **the consultancy gets its own
destination, stated plainly.**

`/now` is retired as an answer to the consultancy question. It stays in the copy library as a
candidate for a secondary route, unresolved, with no claim on the primary navigation.

## 3. Kill criteria — written before anything is scored

Seven tests. Written down first so the scoring cannot be reverse-engineered from a preferred answer.

| # | Test | Fails if |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | **Forty seconds** | A hiring manager cannot name two things the site is offering from the nav alone |
| 2 | **Two doors** | Either audience's first click lands somewhere that reads as *for the other one*, or either is made to feel second-class |
| 3 | **Say it aloud** | Brad would not say the label to a person in a bar |
| 4 | **One beat** | Any label costs the reader a beat of comprehension. Wit yes, riddle no. This is the rule `Unsolicited` broke |
| 5 | **Memorability under contrast** | Nothing is repeatable a day later, *or* everything is odd, which is the same failure |
| 6 | **Only Brad** | The set would work unchanged on somebody else's site |
| 7 | **Bans** | Contains Proof, Unsolicited, consultancy slop, borrowed slang, a formula repeated across labels, or a row composed entirely of abstract category nouns |

Test 7's "formula repeated across labels" clause is the one that does the most killing below, and it
is deliberate. A nav where every item takes the same grammatical shape reads as a device, and a device
is the template failure in a different costume.

## 4. Six systems

Six organising principles, one system each, generated before any were judged. No two share more than
one label.

### A — The reader's job, as verbs

```
Brad Friis        Hire me    Work with me    Check me out    Read me        [ Take the CV — PDF ]
```

| Destination | Gloss |
| --- | --- |
| Hire me | Twenty-five years, the decade selling Schoology and itslearning, and the document you can forward |
| Work with me | Clarity Engine AI. Positioning, commercial setup and customer experience |
| Check me out | Five letters, in full, with names on them |
| Read me | Writing about AI, positioning and customer experience |

**Page headlines.** *Hire me* → "The long version." *Work with me* → "What's stuck?" *Check me out* →

**First clicks.** Recruiter: Hire me. Founder: Work with me. Both doors are explicit and neither is
subordinate.

**For.** The only system where no reader has to translate a category noun into an intention. Both
audiences are named without either being asked to classify themselves.

**Against.** Four first-person verb phrases is a costume, which is exactly test 5's second failure
mode. "Hire me" is needy from a man twenty-five years into a commercial career — it inverts the
status the rest of the site works to establish. And the whole set would run unchanged on any
freelancer's site, which fails test 6 outright.

### C — Hats and roles, the Prolens device made functional

```
Brad Friis        Account manager    Channel partner    Consultant        [ Letters ]  [ CV ]
```

Three hats in the nav, and the same three tags on every career entry, every letter and every article,
so a reader assembles the version of Brad they came for without a switcher and without the site
splitting in two.

**Page headlines.** *Account manager* → "Retention is the whole job." *Channel partner* → "One
territory, ten years, one vendor." *Consultant* → "Tell me what's stuck."

**First clicks.** Recruiter: Account manager or Channel partner. Founder: Consultant.

**For.** It answers the recruiter-versus-founder problem structurally rather than by wording, and it
improves on the template, where the tags are decoration attached to status nouns — *Entrepreneur,
Investor, Advisor* — with nothing behind them. Here every tag is a claim with documents under it.

**Against.** Three roles in a nav bar is a menu of services, which is a freelancer trope wearing a
different hat. Colour-coded pills are the template look this project has already rejected twice, and
without colour the device loses most of its legibility. Worst, it fragments the evidence: the
five-campus save proves all three roles, so tagging it three times makes the tags mean less, not
more. Fails test 6 — the mechanism is the template's, not Brad's.


Folding the CV inside a page rather than a button costs the recruiter a click on the one thing they
came for.

**What survives.** The discipline. Four content routes is the right number, and F proves it.

## 6. Recommendation, and what it costs

```
Brad Friis        The record    Who says so    Consulting    Field notes        [ Take the CV — PDF ]
```

Four content routes, one utility button, ordered by the question the reader is actually asking.
`Contact` lives as the closing block of every page and as a footer link rather than as a nav item,
because a contact tab is the most predictable item in any navigation and this set has one slot to
spend on being unusual. It is already spent on `Who says so`.

**The signature move is `Who says so`, and it is the only one.** Everything else in the row is a
legible signpost. The wit lives one layer down, in the page headlines, the mobile glosses, the CV
button and the 404.

### What it costs, stated plainly

**1. The set is not grammatically consistent.** `The record` takes an article, `Field notes` is a
compound, `Who says so` is a clause and `Consulting` is a bare gerund. A designer will want to
regularise it. Regularising it would create the repeated formula that killed D and F, so the
inconsistency is the price of passing test 7 and it should be defended rather than fixed.

**2. `Consulting` is the plainest word in the row.** Sitting between two characterful neighbours it
can look like a late addition. The alternative — an offer-register label such as `Work with me` —
reads better in the row and worse to a recruiter, and Brad chose plain statement on 11 August. The
mitigation is on the page, not in the label: the consultancy page has to answer *does he actually
want a job* in its first paragraph, or the nav item raises a question the site never closes.

**3. `The record` is cold.** It is confident and faintly official, which suits a career page, but it
has no warmth at all. All the warmth has to come from the page headline, "The long version.", and
from the community material at the foot of the page.

**4. It is not a brave navigation.** One unexpected item in four is a deliberately conservative
setting of the dial, argued from test 5. If Brad wants the site to filter employers rather than
court them, D and E are where to look, and both fail a criterion he set himself. That is the honest
statement of the trade, not a hedge.

**5. `Who says so` gets one shot.** If the letters page underdelivers — three live letters and two
placeholders, no pull quotes, nothing scanned — the boldest label on the site sits over the weakest
page. The label should not ship before the page does.

### 7.1 The capability area — it breaks the site's own rule, so it cannot be a page

Stage 1 §4 sets the governing rule: *no claim appears without its evidence in the same viewport.*

A skills list is claim with no evidence. A page titled `Superpowers`, `Skills` or `What I'm good at`
would be the only page on this site that breaks the rule that makes the site different from every
other personal site in the market. It would also be the page a hiring manager trusts least, because
every candidate has one and every one of them is self-assessed.

`Superpowers` has a second problem Brad should know about: it is common in tech job advertisements
and startup careers pages, so it is considerably less unusual than it feels. It fails test 6.

**The version that works uses the letters.** Five people, across three industries and twenty-five
years, without comparing notes, independently describe the same small number of things. That is not a
claim. That is a converging body of evidence, and it is the most defensible capability statement
available to anyone.

So the capability area is a block, not a route, and it sits on the letters page directly under the
five documents:

> **The three things five people said without comparing notes.**

The three are named from the letters once all five are scanned. On the current three, the candidates
are visible already — adoption by people resistant to it, responsiveness, and translating a platform
into an operating strategy — but that block cannot be written until the advertising and tourism
letters exist, because the whole point is that it is derived rather than asserted. ~~**Blocked on the
two scans.**~~

> **Unblocked 11 August 2026.** All scans read. Three things converge across six references, each
> claimed by at least one 2003–2006 letter and one 2025 letter: **the relationship outlives the
> transaction**, **he makes it work without the standard tooling**, and **he brings the reluctant
> along**. Evidence table in `01d-reference-archive-audit.md` §10; block copy in
> `COPY/navigation/navigation.md`. Note that *without the standard tooling* was not among the three
> predicted above, and *responsiveness* did not survive — it converges across the 2025 letters and
> only weakly before them, which makes it a theme inside one job rather than a pattern across
> twenty-two years. The derived answer differing from the predicted one is the mechanism working.

`Superpowers` stays in the copy library as a candidate for that block's heading, where it is a joke
with evidence under it rather than a claim without any.

### 7.2 The writing area, and the Insights conflict put to Brad rather than dodged

The Stage 1 brief bans `Insights` as consultancy slop in §7. Brad named it directly on 10 August. The
handoff required that this be resolved out loud rather than silently obeyed or silently broken.

**Position: hold the ban, and get the energy from the layer below.**

`Insights` is the single most-used word in New Zealand consultancy marketing. Brad's entire
differentiator, in a market where everyone writes about AI, is that he does not sound like the output
of one — Stage 1 §7 makes that point explicitly about AI writing. A nav label that reads as generated
undermines the material behind it before the reader opens a post.

But the *thing Brad is asking for* is real and the ban does not serve it. He wants that area to sound
thought-provoking and clever, and not so abstract that nobody gets it. Both halves are the brief. The
answer is that the register comes from the article titles and the page headline, which is where a
reader actually experiences cleverness, not from a single word in a 13.5px row.

Six candidates, with costs, all recorded in `COPY/navigation/navigation.md`:

| Label | For | Against |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Field notes** — recommended | Notes from doing the work rather than theorising about it, which is exactly Brad's advantage over every consultant writing about AI. One beat, sayable aloud, thought-provoking without claiming authority | Common in design and user-research circles, so less unusual than it feels. Two words among one-word neighbours |
| **Working it out** | Honest about AI, where nobody has it worked out, and disarming from a senior person. Distinctive | Undersells authority to a founder who is buying judgement. A buyer wants someone who has worked it out |
| **Arguments** | Pointed, thought-provoking, one beat, and it promises a position rather than content | Combative register on a site whose evidence is warmth and patience. Fights the letters |
| **Notes** | Humble, editorial, calm. One beat | Undersells the only asset that works on a stranger, which is the exact failure `Writing` was rejected for |
| **Musings** — Brad's bid | Warm, characterful, unmistakably a person rather than a content function | Reads as idle beside a decade of retention numbers. Fights the seniority the rest of the site builds |
| **Insights** — Brad's bid | Understood everywhere, zero translation cost | Banned §7. The most generic word available in this market |

### 7.3 The role-tag device — adapt, and then defer

**Adopt the idea, reject the mechanism, and do not build it yet.**

The idea worth taking is that one person wears several hats and a reader should be able to assemble
the one they need without a switcher. The mechanism — coloured pills floating round a portrait,
repeated as decoration on every card — is the template look this project has rejected twice, and in
the template the tags are attached to status nouns with nothing behind them.

The honest objection to building it now is arithmetic. A tagging system pays for itself across
volume. This site has one substantial career entry, five letters and three to five articles. There is
almost nothing to tag, and a filter over eleven items is a mechanism that advertises itself and then
underdelivers — the identical failure Stage 1 §6 caught in the lens switcher, and the reason it was
cut. The reason to build it now would be that it is memorable, and that is a reason about the builder
rather than the reader.

**Recommendation:** build the "three things five people said" block instead, which delivers the same
outcome — a reader sees which Brad is relevant to them — from evidence rather than from taxonomy.
Revisit tagging when `Field notes` passes roughly fifteen articles.

### 7.4 Register, specified

Stage 1 specified voice — how sentences are built. It never specified register — how the page holds
itself before a word is read, which is why choosing between the three Stage 3 directions currently
feels like taste.

> **Register: a senior person showing you the documents, not a candidate asking for a chance.**

Three adjectives, each with the thing it must not be mistaken for:

| Is | Is not |
| --- | --- |
| **Plain** | Decorated. No ornament that does not carry information |
| **Warm** | Chummy. Warmth comes from concreteness and from other people's words, never from tone of voice |
| **Unhurried** | Slow. Generous white space and full sentences, but the reader reaches the evidence in one scroll |
