## 1. What this brief decides

Five things, in order of leverage:

1. Who the site is actually for, and what each of those people needs in their first forty seconds.
2. The three-second proposition — one sentence the whole site supports.
3. Proof ordering — which of the four strong assets leads and which is held back.
4. Voice.
5. Whether the audience-lens mechanism survives.

Nothing here is about layout. Layout is Stage 2, and it follows from the hero line rather than the reverse.

## 2. Audiences

Four people land on this site. They arrive by different routes, they read for different lengths of time, and only one of them is looking for a resume.

### 2.1 The hiring manager or internal recruiter — primary

Arrives from a LinkedIn profile link, an application, or a name search after a CV lands on their desk. Hiring for account management, customer success, or a commercial seat in a NZ or AU software business.

They have roughly forty seconds and one question: **is this person going to hit a retention and expansion number, and is there anything here I have not already read on a hundred CVs?**

What they need, in this order: a number they can repeat to their own boss; evidence that the number is real; evidence that a customer would say the same thing unprompted; a way to get the CV without filling in a form.

What loses them: adjectives, a homepage that asks them to make a choice before they know anything, and a resume that is only available as a web page they cannot forward.

### 2.2 The channel or partner lead — secondary, high value

Works at a software vendor, probably offshore, looking for someone to run a NZ or AU territory, or looking at Brad because someone mentioned him. This is a smaller audience than 2.1 but a much better fit for the ten-year channel-partner story, and the roles pay accordingly.

They need different evidence: exclusivity, territory ownership, forecasting, the reporting line back to the vendor, and the fact that the relationship survived ten years and a PowerSchool acquisition. Licensed-user growth matters to them as a territory metric, not a customer-success metric.

This audience is the one genuine argument for tailoring, and section 6 deals with it.

### 2.3 The early-stage NZ software founder — the consulting buyer

Arrives via referral, LinkedIn, or a piece of writing. Considering Clarity Engine AI for positioning, messaging, commercial setup, or a customer-experience audit. They are not buying a CV. They are buying judgement, and the only way to demonstrate judgement before a call is to show the thinking.

They need: proof that Brad has done this recently and specifically (Pikle), a sample of how he reasons (the blog), and a low-friction way to start a conversation that is not an application form.

For this reader the blog is not a nice-to-have. It is the entire sales asset.

### 2.4 The cold reader from an article — the volume audience

Largest by headcount, lowest intent. Arrived at `/writing/[slug]` from LinkedIn, a newsletter, or search. Has never heard of Brad and does not care about his career yet.

They need the article to be good, the page to be fast and readable, and exactly one obvious next action at the end. The conversion here is a follow or a subscribe, not a hire. Anything that treats them like a hiring manager will bounce them.

**Design consequence:** `/writing/[slug]` is not a funnel into the resume. It is its own front door, and it should carry a small, honest author card rather than a pitch.

## 3. The three-second proposition

> **Brad Friis keeps software customers, and grows them, for a decade at a time.**

That is the sentence the site exists to prove. It is not the hero copy — hero copy is section 5 — it is the thing every section has to support or be cut.

Three reasons this is the right proposition:

**It contradicts the category.** Every commercial CV in this market claims to win. Very few claim to keep, because very few can evidence it. Brad can evidence it four separate ways, and one of those ways is a customer writing it down without being asked.

**It is the seat he wants.** The B2B SaaS variant already says it plainly: an account management seat where retention and expansion are the number, not new logos alone. The proposition and the job description are the same sentence.

**It survives all four positionings.** Channel, customer success, professional services and B2B SaaS all reduce to durability. The consulting work is the only outlier, and it inherits credibility from the same claim: someone who held accounts for ten years is a credible person to ask about positioning and customer experience.

### What it is not

It is not "25 years of experience". Twenty-five years is a fact that appears in the support line, never the headline. Longevity on its own reads as age, not as capability, and this market is not always kind about that. Longevity attached to a retained-and-expanded account reads as skill.

Four assets, and the ordering decision is worth more than any other choice on the site. Most personal sites have no third-party proof at all. This one has three written documents and a hard growth number, so the constraint is not scarcity, it is sequencing.

### Rank 1 — The five-campus save. Leads everywhere.

An organisation actively evaluating a move to a competitor stayed, then increased its investment, and its Teaching and Learning Manager wrote it down: *"At a time when we were considering moving away from Schoology, Brad helped us see the platform's potential… giving us the confidence to invest in the platform further."*

This leads because it is the only asset that is simultaneously a story with tension, a commercial outcome, and a third-party document. It proves the hardest thing in account management — retention under live competitive threat — and it proves it in the customer's own words rather than Brad's.

Placement: a named section on the homepage, told in full at roughly 150 words, with the pull-quote inside the same viewport as the claim, and a link to the complete letter.

### Rank 2 — 2,800 to 29,784 licensed users. Hero support line.

The scale number. It sits in the hero support paragraph and in the proof strip immediately below the fold, and it is stated exactly once in each place with no elaboration.

It is ranked second rather than first because it is an unwitnessed claim. A hiring manager cannot check it, and a number with no document behind it is worth less than a smaller outcome with a signature under it. Positioned after the save, it reads as scale. Positioned before, it reads as a CV bullet.

### Rank 3 — The ten-year exclusive vendor relationship. Career section and channel resume.

~~Exclusive reseller agreements in Australia, the United States and Europe~~ — **corrected 12 August 2026.** One reseller agreement, for one product, sold into New Zealand and Australia. Sole territory responsibility, and a relationship that survived a decade and the PowerSchool acquisition of Schoology.

For audience 2.2 this is the single most important fact on the site and it should be near the top of `/resume/channel`. For everyone else it is context that makes rank 1 and rank 2 credible, so on the homepage it belongs in the career block rather than the hero.

### Rank 4 — The Bergh letter. Held for depth, quoted once at the adoption claim.

Nine years, a principal, and the most quotable paragraph anyone has written about Brad: *"One of Brad's most remarkable qualities is his ability to support teachers, particularly those resistant to change."* Also the 95 percent pass rate, the seamless COVID transition, and the nickname "Mister Schoology".

Held back because it is long, sector-specific, and emotionally warm in a way that would soften the top of the page if it led. Its job is to convert the reader who has already decided Brad is competent into someone who wants to work with him. That reader is on `/proof`, not in the hero.

One pull-quote surfaces earlier, adjacent to the adoption and onboarding claim, so the claim is never made without the evidence in the same eyeline.

### Rank 5 — The Rototuna note. Authenticity, not scale.

The smallest document and the most obviously unsolicited. It opens *"I wanted to send a personal note"* and closes *"You're a real star, Brad."*

Its value is that nobody asks for an email like this. Present it verbatim, with the email framing visible — from, to, date — because the format is the proof. Do not clean it up into a testimonial card. The stray colon-bracket smiley stays.

### The rule the ordering implies

**No claim on this site appears without its evidence in the same viewport, and every quote links to the complete document.** A pull-quote with no reachable source is decoration. A pull-quote one click from a signed letter is the reason this site is different from every other personal site in the market.

## 5. Hero copy candidates

Five options. The recommendation is C, and the reasoning for each is the point of this section.

### A — Declarative

> ### I keep software customers.
> Twenty-five years in software, media and agency work, ten of them running a United States vendor's New Zealand and Australian business. The licensed base went from 2,800 to 29,784. Three customers wrote references nobody asked them for.

**For:** Four words, no hedge, and it states the proposition directly. The support paragraph does heavy lifting in one breath and the last sentence is the strongest thing on the site.
**Against:** "Keep" is quiet. A hiring manager scanning for growth verbs may read it as maintenance rather than expansion, which undersells the 30 percent investment lift and the expanded five-campus account.

### B — Story-first

> ### The account that was leaving stayed, then spent more.
> Brad Friis. Account management, customer success and channel partnerships. Auckland.

**For:** The single best sentence Brad owns. It has tension and resolution in eight words and it makes a reader want the next paragraph.
**Against:** It is a case-study headline wearing a hero's clothes. A reader who does not scroll learns nothing about who he is or what he wants, and the name drops to secondary. Better used as the section heading for the save itself, which is where it goes.

### C — Recommended

> ### Twenty-five years selling software. Ten years keeping it sold.
> I ran a United States vendor's New Zealand and Australian business as its only local partner. The licensed base went from 2,800 to 29,784, an account that was actively evaluating a competitor stayed and expanded, and three customers wrote references I never asked for.

**For:** The full proposition in nine words, with a rhythm that survives being repeated. The two halves do different jobs: the first establishes depth, the second names the discipline that most CVs cannot claim. It separates new-logo work from retention and expansion, which is exactly the seat being applied for, and it reads correctly for all four resume positionings without modification. It also sets up the entire page, because everything below it is either selling or keeping.
**Against:** "Keeping it sold" is a shade informal and one conservative reader in ten may find it glib. That risk is acceptable and arguably useful — it sounds like a person rather than a profile.

### D — Number as hero

> ### 2,800 → 29,784
> Brad Friis grew a software vendor's New Zealand and Australian licensed base by 8.75 times in ten years.

**For:** Maximum confidence and it stops the eye. Photographs well.
**Against:** The number means nothing until the support line explains it, so the three-second read fails. It also reduces a twenty-five year career to one metric and makes the site brittle: the moment Brad wants a role where that number is not the relevant proof, the whole hero is wrong. Discard.

### E — Adoption angle

> ### I get non-technical people to actually use the software they paid for.
> Twenty-five years in software and services. Ten of them running a US vendor's New Zealand and Australian business, where the job was less about the licence and more about the people who had to open it on Monday morning.

**For:** The most specific and most human option, and it is the thing both written references independently describe. Very hard to fake, and it would win the professional-services and customer-success roles outright.
**Against:** It narrows. It reads as an onboarding and adoption specialist, which quietly closes the door on channel roles and on account management seats carrying a number. The idea is too good to lose, so it moves: it becomes the heading of the homepage section where the Bergh quote sits.

### Recommendation

**C as the display line, with A's final sentence folded into the support paragraph** — as written above. Then E's idea survives as a section heading further down the page, and B's line survives as the heading of the case study. Nothing good is wasted; the three ideas are sequenced rather than competing for the same 900 pixels.

## 6. The audience-lens hypothesis — tested, and mostly rejected

The proposal was a lens control that rewrites the hero subheading, re-ranks achievements, re-orders case studies and filters the writing feed, held in the URL as `/?lens=channel`.

Four of the twelve evidence rows are identical across every variant, and those four include the three strongest assets. The B2B SaaS and professional-services variants share every substantive bullet and differ only in emphasis and adjective. Ecommerce differs solely because of the current Pikle engagement. Channel is the only variant that swaps in a genuinely different job family: vendor-side rather than customer-side.

**Result: the hypothesis fails as stated.** Roughly seventy-five percent of the content is shared. Three of the four lenses would re-order the same six achievements, and a reader would toggle between them and see almost nothing move. A control that promises personalisation and then delivers a reshuffle is worse than no control, because it advertises a capability and then underdelivers it in the reader's first ten seconds.

### The second, larger problem

A visible lens control asks the reader to classify themselves before they have any context. A hiring manager does not think of themselves as a "channel lens" — they think of themselves as someone filling a Partner Manager role at a specific company, and they will not spend a decision on a pill row above the fold. The control costs attention at the exact moment attention is most expensive, and it optimises for the reader Brad least needs to convince.

### What survives, and why

**Keep the URL parameter. Drop the visible control.**

`?lens=` is genuinely useful in one direction only: outbound. Brad applies for a channel role and sends `bradfriis.co/resume/channel`. The link arrives pre-framed, which was the good idea inside the original hypothesis, and it works precisely because *Brad* sets the lens, not the reader. The reader never has to know a mechanism exists.

And it belongs on the resume rather than the homepage, because a resume is a document read by one person for one specific role. That is where tailoring pays. A homepage is read by all four audiences and has to hold a single, confident position — which section 3 already establishes it can, since all four positionings reduce to the same proposition.

### The recommendation in full

1. **One homepage hero for everyone.** The retention proposition holds across all four positionings, so there is nothing to switch.
2. **`/resume` and `/resume/[lens]`** — four routes, `master`, `b2b-saas`, `channel`, `customer-success`. Content already exists; the marginal build cost is a Sanity `lens` reference on `role` and `achievement`, which the content model already anticipates. Highest value per unit of work on the site.
3. **Replace the homepage lens control with a three-card "What I'm here for" block** — Account management and customer success · Channel and partnership · AI and product consulting. These route to depth rather than re-rendering the page, they tell all four audiences within one scroll that they are in the right place, and they cost the reader nothing.
4. **Keep filtering where it is genuinely useful:** topic filters on `/writing`, because a reader browsing an archive has already committed and a filter there saves them work rather than demanding a decision.

The Prolens pill visual is discarded along with the mechanism. It was a static tag in the template and making it functional was the tempting move; the reason to make it functional was that it would be memorable, which is a reason about the builder rather than the reader.

**Consequence for the plan:** the `lens-switcher` task in Phase B shrinks from cross-page state management to a route parameter on one page. Retire the todo as written and replace it with resume-variant routing.

## 7. Voice

The reference letters describe warmth, patience, humour, humility and generosity. Every commercial CV in this market flattens exactly those qualities out, which makes them a differentiator rather than a liability.

**Write as Brad would speak to a customer he respects.** First person. Present tense. Short sentences that do not perform confidence, because the numbers are doing that already.

Rules:

- **Specific over impressive.** "An account that was actively evaluating a competitor" beats "a challenging retention scenario". Every time.
- **State numbers once, plainly.** 2,800 to 29,784. Thirty percent. Ten years. No exclamation, no "an incredible", no rounding up to make a better rhythm.
- **Let the customers do the praising.** Brad never writes that he is patient or trusted. Adrian Pilgrim and Andrew Bergh already did, in writing, and quoting them is worth more than any self-description.
- **Warmth through concreteness, humour through restraint.** One dry line per page at most. The Rototuna smiley does more work than any joke Brad could write.
- **Banned:** passionate, results-driven, proven track record, journey, thrilled, excited to share, leveraged, synergy, "I'm not just X, I'm Y", and any sentence a hundred other candidates could have written.
- **New Zealand English throughout.** Organise, recognise, colour, centre, programme, licence as noun and license as verb. Dates as 10 August 2026. No serial comma unless the sentence breaks without it.
- **On AI:** Brad writes about AI for business. The fastest way to lose that audience is to sound like the output of one. Prefer the concrete engagement over the general claim, and never open a post with "In today's rapidly evolving landscape".

### 7.1 Visual bans — added 12 August 2026 from the strategy interview

The bans above govern words. These govern behaviour, and they come from questions 11, 12 and 14 of
§11. They are testable, which is the only reason they are worth writing down.

- **Every surprise carries information.** Motion or reveal that delivers a fact is allowed. Motion
  that announces itself is decoration and is banned. This is what reconciles Brad admiring quirk and
  surprise (`erikkindel.com`) with Brad detesting animation overload (`anthonydesigner.com`). The
  test for any proposed effect is one question: **what does the reader know afterwards that they did
  not know before?** If the answer is *that the site can do that*, it is cut.
- **No scroll-jacked or fixed side frames.** Brad named the pattern specifically — the right-hand
  scrolling frame at `nathanielkoloc.com/venture-building`. Scroll position belongs to the reader.
  This bans scroll hijacking, pinned side panels that advance on scroll, and any full-page section
  snapping.

Two further constraints from the same interview sit here because they are enforced visually rather
than editorially:

- **Under 250 words above the fold.** Written 12 August as a direct instruction about the copy, not
  the layout — two of Brad's three repellent references failed on wordiness.

  **Fold scope resolved, 17 August 2026, by Brad, from a screenshot of the live page.** The 17
  August narrowing had defined "above the fold" as hero + stats + capabilities, on the reasoning
  that capabilities is still part of the forty-second read. Measured against that definition the
  block ran 318 words — a 68-word breach, concentrated in the capability cells (223 words against a
  175-word remainder). Brad looked at the actual rendered first viewport and ruled that definition
  wrong: **the capability cards sit below the stats band and are not visible without scrolling.**
  The literal fold is hero + stats only, which measures ~70 words. **No breach exists, and none
  ever did** — the capabilities section was never above the fold at the current breakpoint. The
  250-word cap stands, governing hero + stats exactly as rendered, with headroom to spare.
  Capabilities and everything below it is below-fold and uncapped, per the original 17 August
  reasoning that a reader who scrolls has already opted in.

  This also resolves the standing whole-page question: since there was no real breach to chase,
  there is nothing forcing a whole-page cap either. The three prior whole-page figures (~720, ~847,
  ~1,027) disagreed by up to 40%; a clean re-measurement on 17 August put visible body copy at 845
  words, and Brad has not asked for a page-wide ceiling. The banned-word list, NZ English, and the
  rest of §7 are unaffected.
- **Relationships and win/win are a structure, never a sentence.** The belief is legitimate and the
  vocabulary is not: *win/win* is the same class of word as *synergy*, and *a real desire to* is a
  self-adjective already banned above. It is carried by the fact that six people wrote it down and
  one of them nine years after Brad left. **Proven by who stayed, never stated.**

## 8. The two open content questions

### 8.1 "What I'm proud of" — recommend cutting the section as templated

The Prolens template fills this with family photographs and side projects. There is no material for that in the resumes and Brad has not offered any, so the honest options are to invent something, to use family, or to change what the section is.

**Recommendation: cut it from the homepage and replace it with nothing.** The homepage already runs long, and the strongest "proud of" material Brad has is not a project category — it is outcomes involving people, and those are already covered better elsewhere: the account that stayed, the teachers who were resistant to change and became confident users, the 95 percent pass rate at a school that was ready for COVID because someone had planned for it two years earlier.

**What moves instead:** a short, unheadlined block on `/about` covering the board work at Foodbank Hawke's Bay and Volunteering New Zealand, and the Bergh letter's line about agency, equity and reducing teacher workload. That is the character evidence, it is already documented, and on `/about` it reaches the reader who is looking for it without slowing down the reader who is not.

**If Brad wants the section kept**, the version that works is a single block titled "Work I'd do again" holding three things — the five-campus unified strategy, the COVID-readiness planning, and the Foodbank board seat — with no photographs. Family stays off a site whose primary job is winning commercial roles, unless Brad specifically wants it there, in which case it belongs on `/about` at the bottom and not on the homepage.

**Decision needed from Brad.** Default assumed for Stage 2: cut from homepage, community and governance folded into `/about`.

### 8.2 `/proof` as destination or distributed — recommend both, with a strict rule

Not an either/or. Each does a job the other cannot.

**Distributed** is where persuasion happens. A quote next to the claim it supports is evidence; a quote in a carousel three screens later is decoration. So: the Pilgrim quote sits inside the account-save section, the Bergh change-resistance quote sits beside the adoption claim, and the Rototuna note sits near the responsiveness claim on `/about`.

**`/proof` is where credibility is settled.** A pull-quote is only worth something if the full document is one click away, and a page holding three complete, signed, named references is a thing no competing personal site in this market has. It is the single most defensible page on the site and it should be in the primary navigation, not buried.

**The rule:** every distributed quote is a link to its own anchor on `/proof`, labelled with what the reader will get — "Read Adrian Pilgrim's full reference" rather than "Learn more". Full letters, named authors, roles, dates, and the original PDFs available for download. Nothing anonymised, nothing trimmed to flatter.

### 11.1 What the interview overturned

Five things. The first two are factual errors that had been propagating since stage 1.

**Correction 1 — the reseller story is not what §3.1 says it is.** §3.1 filed the reseller agreements
as *three territories that did not exist as revenue until he negotiated them*, originated in AU, the
US and Europe. That is wrong. Brad **managed one reseller agreement, for one product, and sold it
into New Zealand and Australia.** There were no sub-resellers beneath him. What it evidences is
**contracts, pricing matrices and international negotiation**, and nothing about building a channel.
The *best origination evidence in the archive* claim is void. Corrected in §3.1, §4 rank 3 and the
§6 table.

**Correction 2 — the employer is never named, on the site or on the CV.** Brad's decision, for
reasons that are his. The replacement framing, which he supplied and which was tightened for
accuracy:

> **Ten years selling PowerSchool Schoology and itslearning into New Zealand and Australia as the
> vendor's local channel partner.**

**Never *working with PowerSchool*.** He was not employed by them, and that reading collapses on a
reference check. This is a net gain rather than a cost: PowerSchool and Schoology are names a hiring
manager recognises, and the employer's name is not. Brad also chose the unnamed-employer line on the
CV, having been told what it costs — it reads as contract or self-employed and invites its own
questions.

**Correction 3 — sales enablement is restored, and decision 4 of the 11 August handoff is
overturned.** Brad first answered that he had no nameable instance. He then named four, unprompted.

| Instance | What it is |
| --- | --- |
| **PowerSchool** | Trained on the vendor's enablement programme as the local partner, then **rebuilt it for New Zealand and Australia** — a programme written for another market, reworked for this one |
| **99 Corporation** (direct marketing) | **Built the sales ramp from nothing.** No onboarding sequence for new salespeople existed; he created one |
| **Adplus** and **Xplore** | Sales strategy developed in role. Adplus is where AR Walker wrote the 24 March 2003 strike-rate letter, so the strategy and its evidence are the same fifteen months |

Asked which single instance had the clearest before-and-after, Brad said *each business was
different, though most certainly there are main similarities.* **The similarities are the method, and
that is the strongest answer of the session.** A ramp derived from one company is an anecdote; one
derived from four businesses across direct marketing, advertising and an enterprise software channel
is a method. The instance count is the evidence.

*Why it is claimable now when decision 4 said it was not.* Decision 4 was written under the
achievement-led architecture, where *I improved a team's sales process* requires a before-and-after
number. Under the knowledge-led architecture at §11.2, *here is how I ready a person to represent a
product and sell on value* is a **method, shown** — self-evidencing, because the reader judges it on
the page. Nothing is asserted about how good Brad is, so §7 is satisfied. **Brad chose *as method,
shown*:** the sequence leads; PowerSchool, 99 Corporation, Adplus and Xplore appear underneath as
where it was used, never as the claim.

*What is still banned.* *Developed all sales strategy at every role* and *outstanding sales strategy
skills* remain unusable — unfalsifiable self-assessment, §7 class. **Show the method; never assert
the competence.**

**Correction 4 — the failure is legally excluded, permanently.** The bet that did not pay is subject
to a settlement Brad is not permitted to discuss. This is a hard constraint, not a judgement call,
and it is recorded here **so that no future session goes fishing for it.** Separately, and before
learning this, the recommendation for a published *what I got wrong* section was withdrawn on its own
merits: it is LinkedIn genre, and the verbal and visual bans exist to kill conventions adopted
because they are conventions.

**Correction 5 — one usable fact survives, outside the gag.** Brad negotiated distribution agreements
for **two further products** and travelled overseas twice to onboard and learn them. Stated as
capability and stopped there — no outcome, no aftermath — it touches nothing the settlement covers,
and it is better commercial-terms evidence than the Schoology agreement. **Brad holds the settlement
wording and must clear the exact sentence before it ships.**
