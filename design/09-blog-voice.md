# 09 — Blog voice

**Written:** Saturday 15 August 2026
**Governs:** every post in the blog, and nothing else.
**Does not govern:** the homepage, the career record, `/letters`, the CV, navigation. Those are §7 of
`design/01-positioning-brief.md` and they do not move.

---

## 0. What this document is, and why it is not §7.2

Every other writing document in this project **generates** copy and hands Brad options to choose
between. This one does not. Brad's instruction, 15 August 2026, verbatim:

> "I want the blog to be open to my own tone and manner in terms of writing style."
>
> "Though if we generated a prompt that could pass over writing before it is added, that would be
> good."

So the blog inverts the project's normal direction of work. **Brad writes the post. This document
checks it afterwards.** That is why it is a separate file rather than §7.2 of the positioning brief:
§7 is a set of bans on copy an agent drafts, and folding the blog into it would imply those bans
apply, which Brad has explicitly said they do not.

The deliverable is **§4, the review prompt**. Sections 1–3 exist to make §4 defensible; §4 is the
thing that gets run.

**One thing this document must not become.** A review instrument applied hard enough stops being a
check and becomes the style cage Brad ruled out. Every flag in §4 is therefore **a question raised,
not a change made** — and §4.4 says what to do when Brad overrules a flag, which is: record it and
publish anyway.

---

## 1. The blog is a sales surface — which sets how permissive this spec can be

Last session Brad was told the blog was a room a reader visits *after* being convinced, so a second
voice there was low risk. **He overturned that**, 15 August 2026:

> "I want the blog to be linked from the main page as I disagree it stays silent — want to push
> people to it to see my intellect and insights — this will help sell the broadness of my knowledge
> and help to generate the goal of seeing me as a thought leader."

The consequence is not stylistic, it is structural: **the same hiring reader who reads the career
record reads the blog, in the same visit, from a link Brad has asked to put above the fold.** There
is no audience separation to hide behind.

**What this rules out:** any rule in this document justified on the grounds that blog readers are
less critical, arrive warmer, or matter less than homepage readers. None of that is true any more.

**What this does not rule out:** a different register. A hiring reader can meet a different *voice*
on the blog without harm. What they cannot meet is a *lower standard* — the cliché, the unevidenced
figure, the sentence a hundred other candidates could have written. Those damage the career record
retroactively, because the reader does not maintain separate credibility ledgers for two pages of
the same site.

**The line this spec draws: register is free, discipline is not.**

## 2. Which §7 rules survive into the blog

§7 has seven word-rules and three behaviour-rules. They do not travel as a block, and the reason
each one exists determines whether it survives.

| §7 rule | Survives? | Why |
| --- | :---: | --- |
| **NZ English throughout** — organise, colour, centre, licence/license, dates as 10 August 2026, no serial comma unless needed | **Yes, unchanged** | This is orthography, not voice. A blog spelling it *color* on a site spelling it *colour* looks like it was written by someone else — which, to a reader assessing whether Brad writes his own material, is the worst possible implication. |
| **Banned words** — passionate, results-driven, proven track record, journey, thrilled, excited to share, leveraged, synergy, "I'm not just X, I'm Y" | **Yes, unchanged — and it binds harder here** | See §2.1. |
| **No invented figures; every number traceable** | **Yes, and it binds hardest of all** | See §2.2. |
| **Let the customers do the praising** — Brad never writes that he is patient or trusted | **Yes, in substance** | A thought-leadership post is a self-assertion by construction, so this cannot mean *never claim anything*. It means: **claim about the work, not about the self.** "Here is how I ran a five-campus save" is allowed. "I am the kind of person who saves accounts" is the banned move, on any page. |
| **On AI: never sound like the output of one** | **Yes, and it is now load-bearing** | §7 wrote this as a caution. The blog has an entire AI category, so it becomes an operating rule. See row 3 of §3. |
| **First person, present tense** | **No** | A sales surface describes standing capability, so present tense is right there. A blog post narrates completed events. The Cubs story happened in the past and forcing it into present tense is a fiction-writing affectation, not a house style. First person survives as a default, not a rule — a post can quote, report, or run second person. |
| **Short sentences that do not perform confidence** | **No** | The clause after the comma is the actual rule: *because the numbers are doing that already.* On the homepage the numbers carry the confidence, so the prose must not. A blog post arguing a position has no numbers doing that work, and the argument has to carry itself. This is the rule the fiction register directly contradicts, and it is correctly dropped. |
| **One dry line per page at most** | **No** | A rationing rule for a page where humour is a seasoning. On the blog, humour is sometimes the whole dish — Brad named "the odd quirky story" as a category. Rationing it to one line per post would gut row four. |
| **Under 250 words** | **No** | A homepage constraint about a reader deciding whether to stay. It has no meaning on a page they have already chosen to read. **But see §5 — it governs the homepage link, which is not a blog post.** |
| **Every surprise carries information** | **Yes, translated** | §7.1 wrote this about motion and reveal. Translated to prose: **a digression must pay.** A tangent that delivers a fact, a mechanism or a reversal earns its place; one that only demonstrates that Brad can write is decoration. Same test, same question: *what does the reader know afterwards that they did not know before?* |

### 2.1 Why the banned-word list survives intact

This is the one that looks arguable, and it is not.

The list reads as a CV-cliché list, so the instinct is that it is a CV-specific rule. **The opposite
is true.** *Journey*, *passionate*, *proven track record* and *I'm not just X, I'm Y* are not
primarily CV words — they are **LinkedIn thought-leadership words**. Their native habitat is exactly
the kind of post Brad is proposing to write. A blog whose stated purpose is *"to see my intellect
and insights"* is the single worst place on this site to deploy the vocabulary that signals the
absence of both.

There is also a mechanical argument. Brad has asked for the blog to demonstrate range. **Range is
demonstrated by specificity** — four categories that each sound different. The banned words are
generic by definition; they are what every category sounds like when the writer has nothing
particular to say. Using them collapses the four registers into one, which defeats the stated
purpose of having four.

**Ruling: the §7 banned list applies to the blog in full, with no exceptions and no per-category
carve-outs.** It is the only §7 word-rule that survives *unchanged in kind* rather than translated.

**Additions for the blog specifically**, because these are thought-leadership tells §7 never needed
to ban:

- *In today's rapidly evolving landscape* and every variant. §7 already names this one.
- *Let that sink in.* / *Read that again.*
- *Here's the thing:* as a paragraph opener.
- *Game-changer*, *unlock*, *supercharge*, *10x*, *deep dive*, *at scale* used as a verb-adjacent
  boast rather than a description of actual scale.
- **The one-sentence-paragraph cascade.** Four consecutive paragraphs of under eight words each, used
  for emphasis. This is a LinkedIn formatting tic, and it reads as performance rather than thought.
- *The best salespeople know…* / *Great account managers do X.* Universal claims about a profession,
  offered as insight. Brad's authority is that he did specific things at named accounts; a universal
  claim trades that authority for a platitude.

### 2.2 Why the figures rule binds hardest here

On the homepage, every figure passed through the claims register before it was placed, and there are
perhaps a dozen of them. **A blog post is written in Brad's own voice, quickly, from memory, and
memory is exactly where the barred figures live.** The 400 per cent is dead but Brad said it once and
believed it. The 60/40 Visitor Guide reconciliation is unresolved. Deane Jessep, 95bFM and the
Canwest sales figures are permanently unpublishable.

None of those constraints are visible to someone typing a post at speed. **This is the single
highest-risk property of the blog** and it is the reason §4.2 is a hard-stop check rather than a
flag.

## 3. The register taxonomy

Brad named four categories, 15 August 2026:

> "There will be several styles of article in the blog — web strategy, communications and business
> identity, AI — several topics related to business, and of course the odd quirky story like the
> cubs and plastic bags."

Four rows. Each has a different reader, a different job, and a different failure mode. **The failure
column is the useful one** — it is what §4 checks against.

### Row 1 — Web strategy

- **What it is:** how sites are actually built to sell — structure, evidence placement, what a
  hiring or buying reader does on a page, why a portfolio pattern fails a sales job.
- **Reader:** someone evaluating whether Brad understands the medium he is being assessed in.
- **Authority comes from:** this site. Brad has ten sessions of documented decisions with arguments
  for and against. **This is the only category where the proof is the page the reader is standing
  on**, which makes it the strongest of the four and the one to lead with.
- **Fails when:** it becomes generic web-design advice available from any agency blog. The test:
  **could this post have been written by someone who had not built this site?** If yes, cut it.
- **Voice:** closest to §7 of the four. Concrete, specific, minimal.

### Row 2 — Communications and business identity

- **What it is:** brand governance, message discipline, why an organisation says different things in
  different rooms and what it costs.
- **Reader:** a commercial reader — the person who would hire Brad into an account role.
- **Authority comes from:** Xplore's branding and copy service line, Tracta's Gold Pages tiering, the
  99 Corporation campaign. **Named work, not theory.**
- **Fails when:** it argues from principle rather than from an engagement. A post on brand
  consistency with no named account behind it is a LinkedIn post, and Brad's advantage is that he
  has the accounts.
- **Voice:** first person, past tense, one engagement per post rather than a survey.

### Row 3 — AI

- **What it is:** AI applied to commercial workflow — what Brad has actually run, what broke, what
  it cost.
- **Reader:** the most sceptical of the four, and the most likely to be reading to catch him out.
- **Authority comes from:** the Clarity Engine and Pikle work — **and it is thin, deliberately.**
  §7 already warns about this audience; the standing constraint is harder: **nothing may imply an AI
  product has shipped.** Clarity Engine is unregistered, the Pikle project is paused, and the work
  predates any market result.
- **Fails when:** (a) it implies a shipped product, which is a hard stop, not a flag; (b) it reads as
  AI-generated, which on an AI post is fatal in a way it is nowhere else; (c) it makes general
  predictions about AI's trajectory. **Brad's credibility here is entirely as a practitioner and
  entirely zero as a futurist.** A post about what he ran on a Tuesday is strong. A post about where
  AI is going in three years is worthless and actively damaging, because a thousand better-placed
  people have written it.
- **Voice:** the most restrained of the four. Concrete engagement, stated limits, no forecast.

### Row 4 — The quirky story

- **What it is:** the childhood commercial stories — Cubs, the plastic bags — and their kin.
- **Reader:** everyone. This is the category most likely to be read to the end and most likely to be
  remembered.
- **Authority comes from:** it being true and being his.
- **This row and only this row is written in the fiction register.** Brad tabled a high-craft
  fiction style guide plus two worked examples — currently at
  `COPY/blog/Nobunaga writing style example` and `COPY/blog/Nobunga writing example 2`, which is
  the wrong folder for them (see §7). **The examples are the better spec; read them before drafting
  and prefer them over any prose description of the style, including §3.1 below.** The register is
  the spec for row four and it does not touch rows one to three.
- **Fails when:** it states its own moral. See §3.1.
- **Voice:** total seriousness applied to a small subject. The collision is the engine. **See §3.1 —
  the register is deadpan, not grand.**

### 3.1 The fiction register — what carries into row four

**Corrected 15 August 2026 after reading Brad's two tabled examples** —
`COPY/blog/Nobunaga writing style example` and `COPY/blog/Nobunga writing example 2`. The
earlier assessment described the register from a summary and got one thing wrong; the correction is
in the first bullet.

- **The register is deadpan literal, not "elevated, formal, classical, solemn."** The examples do
  not run grand diction. *"I have held down eleven forts."* *"I identified the enemy. It was a
  raccoon."* Those are flat declaratives. **The comedy is that the narrator is entirely sincere and
  never winks**, and the sentences are mostly short. This matters practically: it means the register
  is far closer to Brad's natural speech than a "classical" instruction would suggest, and it means
  **the §7 rule about not performing confidence was never really in conflict** — the fiction voice
  performs *seriousness*, which is a different thing. Do not bolt grandeur onto these stories.
- **The high/low collision is the right engine for both stories.** Both are a child running a
  commercial operation with complete seriousness. The credibility and the comedy come from the same
  fact: **the kid was not joking.** A beetroot tin and a box of freezer bags treated with the
  gravity of a campaign is funny *because* it is also accurate.
- **The ending is an object plus a refusal, and no explanation.** Both examples land this way — the
  FORT COMMANDER badge (*"I did not ask for this rank. I will not give it back."*), the shivering
  narrator stepping back into August. **The interviews supplied the equivalent object for each
  story**: the leather bobble and the lifelong refusal to wear branded clothing (Cubs), the Casio
  sitting unused on a shelf (bags). Neither may be explained.
- **"Emotion via concrete objects" is already how the best material on this site works.** The tin
  can. The mother making him return every dollar. The Rototuna smiley. This is not a new technique
  being imported; it is the existing technique named.
- **"No stated morals" is the rule that earns its place**, and it is a genuine correction rather than
  a preference. `design/01-positioning-brief.md:149` runs three sentences of Cubs story and then
  explains itself: *"asking is not the embarrassing part. Asking badly is."* The explanation is what
  makes it read as juvenile — it does not trust the reader with a story a reader can obviously
  parse. **Cutting the moral is what makes the piece adult.** The 15 August interview went further:
  it produced a *different and better* ending for Cubs, which makes the moral not merely banned but
  redundant. See `COPY/interviews/7-childhood-cubs-uniform.md`.
- **The four-beat arc (entry → repetition → naming → belonging) fits neither story as predicted.**
  It does not fit Cubs, which is one transaction and a reversal — **let the Cubs piece be short and
  break the arc.** And the 15 August interview establishes it does not fit the bags either in the
  way that was assumed: **there were no repeat customers and no route.** Brad: *"it was a single
  sale product. Once a signature was colected I promptly moved on."* The repetition beat exists, but
  it is the 6–7km ride home every evening, not returning to buyers. **Do not manufacture repeat
  business to fill a structural slot.**

- **The one real conflict**, rule 2's "elevated, formal, classical, solemn internal voice" against
  §7's "short sentences that do not perform confidence" — resolved by §2 above, which drops that §7
  rule for the blog on its own merits. The conflict does not survive into this spec.

## 4. The review prompt

**This is the deliverable.** Run over a finished draft, by an agent or by Brad. It produces **flags,
not rewrites.** It never edits the post.

> **Instructions to the reviewer.** You are checking a blog post Brad has written for
> `bradfriis.com`. **You are not editing it and you are not improving it.** Your entire output is a
> list of flags with locations and one-line reasons, plus a verdict. Brad's own voice is the point of
> this blog; a sentence you would have written differently is not a flag. Only the checks below are
> flags. If a check finds nothing, say so in one line and move on. Read `design/09-blog-voice.md` §2
> and §3 and `COPY/interviews/_claims-register.md` before starting.

### 4.1 Hard stops — publication blockers

Any hit here blocks the post until Brad rules on it. These are not style flags; they are the
standing constraints from the project record, and they apply to blog posts exactly as they apply to
every other surface.

1. **The education employer named.** The 2015–2025 employer is never named in site output. Check for
   the company name, and for the indirect identification — a unique combination of product,
   territory and date that names it without naming it.
2. **The settlement gag.** Any reference to the dispute that ended the 2015–2025 role. Do not steer
   there and do not permit an oblique reference.
3. **Deane Jessep, 95bFM, or the Canwest sales figures.** Permanently unpublishable, by name or by
   description.
4. **The 400 per cent (Canwest sales).** Dead and does not revive. **Note the trap:** the $400k
   MySimpleSiteMan figure is a different row on a different topic and is not barred. Both contain
   "400" — check which one is present before flagging.
5. **Anything implying an AI product has shipped.** Clarity Engine is unregistered; the Pikle project
   is paused; the work predates any market result. Present tense about a product — *Clarity Engine
   does X* — is the failure mode. This bites hardest on row-three posts.
6. **Not publishable by default:** the achilles injury that ended 99 Corporation; the founder's
   personal issue that paused Pikle.

### 4.2 Figures — check every number, no exceptions

For **every** figure, percentage, count, duration and dollar amount in the post:

1. **Is it in `COPY/interviews/_claims-register.md`?** If it is not in the register, flag it. It is
   not necessarily wrong — it may be new, and new material is legitimate — but it has not been
   through the register and it goes to Brad before it publishes.
2. **Does it match the register's wording**, or has it drifted? A figure that has changed magnitude,
   unit or timeframe between the register and the post is a flag even when the change looks
   trivial.
3. **Is it a derived figure?** **Never combine, divide or compound two separately-given figures into
   a third.** 120 ÷ 70, $70k + $30k + $25k, 5 → 12, $70k annualised and the 60/40 reconciliation are
   barred by row. If the post contains arithmetic the register does not contain, flag it — this is
   the most common way a barred figure gets in, because it does not appear in the source.
4. **Is the claim it supports the same claim the register supports?** A true figure attached to a
   different assertion is a new claim.

**Precedent, and it stands:** a dictated figure that disagrees with the register may be a
correction or a misspeak. **Ask which. Do not assume either.**

### 4.3 Style flags — questions, not corrections

1. **Banned words.** The §7 list plus the §2.1 blog additions. Quote the sentence and name the word.
   No exceptions, no per-category carve-outs.
2. **NZ English.** Spelling, dates as *15 August 2026*, *itslearning* always lower case.
3. **Self-praise.** Any sentence where Brad states a quality of himself rather than an action he
   took or a result that occurred. *Claim about the work, not about the self.*
4. **Category failure.** Identify which of the four rows the post belongs to, then run **that row's
   failure test from §3** and no other row's:
   - Row 1 — could this have been written by someone who had not built this site?
   - Row 2 — is there a named engagement behind the argument, or is it principle only?
   - Row 3 — does it forecast? Does it imply a shipped product? Does it read as machine-written?
   - Row 4 — does it state its moral?
5. **Row four only: the stated moral.** The specific test — **does the piece explain itself after
   telling itself?** Look for the closing paragraph that begins *What I learned was* or *The lesson
   there is*, and for the sentence that converts the story into a transferable business principle.
   That sentence is the flag, and cutting it is almost always the fix.
6. **Decoration.** Any digression, flourish or reveal that does not deliver a fact, a mechanism or a
   reversal. The §7.1 test, applied to prose: **what does the reader know afterwards that they did
   not know before?** If the answer is *that Brad can write*, flag it.
7. **The generic-post test**, over the whole piece: **could a hundred other candidates have written
   this?** This is §7's own final banned-words clause applied at the level of the post rather than
   the sentence, and it is the check most likely to catch a post that passes every other test and
   still should not publish.

### 4.4 Output format

```text
VERDICT: publish / publish after Brad rules / blocked

HARD STOPS (n)
  [location] — [which constraint] — [quoted text]

FIGURES (n)
  [location] — [figure] — in register / not in register / derived / drifted from row X

STYLE FLAGS (n)
  [location] — [check] — [quoted text] — [one-line reason]

CATEGORY: row 1 / 2 / 3 / 4 — [row failure test result]

NOTHING FOUND: [list of checks that came back clean]
```

**When Brad overrules a flag, the flag was still correct to raise.** Record the overrule in the
post's `COPY/blog/` entry as an argument-against, per the standing four-status convention, and
publish. **A style flag is never a veto.** Only §4.1 blocks, and even then it blocks pending Brad's
ruling rather than absolutely — with the exception of the settlement gag, which is not Brad's to
waive unilaterally and should be raised with him rather than resolved in a draft.

## 5. The homepage link is not a blog post, and §7 governs it

Brad wants the blog linked from the homepage. **That link is homepage copy**, so §7 applies to it in
full — including the under-250-words cap and the every-element-carries-information test.

**"Read my blog" carries no information and fails §7.1 on its own terms.** So does "Insights",
"Thoughts" and every other label of that class.

The link has to **teach the reader something on the way past**. The candidates, in rough order of
how much they cost the word budget:

- A real post title, doing real work, updated as posts publish.
- A title plus a one-line standfirst.
- A named topic list — the four rows as four words — which sells range, which is Brad's stated
  reason for wanting the link.

**This is a design problem, not a copy problem, and it is not solved in this document.** It needs a
pass of its own, and `COPY/Blog-Insights-Shared-Wisdom.png` is an existing visual mock to reconcile
against before designing from scratch.

## 6. Open — Brad's call, not resolved here

**The existing Cubs paragraph in About Me.** `design/01-positioning-brief.md:36` and `:119` assign it
a specific job: *"a short, memorable origin point for Brad's sales instinct."* That job is done by
its closing moral — *asking is not the embarrassing part, asking badly is* — which is precisely what
§3.1 bans from a row-four post.

**Both cannot be true.** Either the About Me paragraph keeps the moral and the blog post is a
different piece of writing about the same event, or the paragraph goes and the blog post carries the
story alone. Put to Brad; not resolved in this spec.
