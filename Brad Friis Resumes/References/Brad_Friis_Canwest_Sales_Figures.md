## Two corrections to what was assumed going in

**1. "172% of quarter target" is not what the sheet says, and the claim must not be used.**
September is zero for every person on the sheet, including the team average. That is not a quarter
in which one month failed — it is a quarter that had not happened yet. The 172% is a cumulative
figure over **two months** of a three-month period. Stating it as a quarter result overstates it, and
it overstates it on the face of the very document offered as proof. A numerate reader checks the
image, finds the zero row, and stops trusting the page. This is the worst possible failure mode: a
false claim printed directly above its own refutation.

**2. "Highest of seven" is true, and it is a margin of one percentage point.**
Brad 172%, next 171%. The phrase is accurate and it is also, on this sheet, close to meaningless.
Published as a headline it is the kind of statistic that reads as impressive until someone looks,
and then reads as spin. The sheet's July column is a genuinely commanding result — 102% against a
team average of 78%, the only person over target. The cumulative column is a photo finish. Using the
weaker number because it is the bigger one is the trade this document invites and it should be
declined.

## What the sheet does support, cleanly

**Brad was the only one of seven salespeople with no shortfall against the July budget.**
Every other row in the shortfall table carries a dollar figure. Brad's carries a dash. Seven people
were $43,198 short of a $35,000 monthly budget between them, and Brad contributed nothing to that gap.

That is one sentence, it needs no averaging, no cumulative arithmetic and no quarter framing, and it
is the strongest thing on the page.

## Recommendation: withhold the image. Take the fact, from the letter, not the sheet

**Withhold, and do not publish a redacted version either.**

The argument the previous session made stands and the numbers strengthen it. The sheet names six
identifiable people, in a small city, in a small industry, and shows most of them missing budget. One
of them missed by $13,713 against a $35,000 target. Publishing that — at any level of redaction —
puts twenty-year-old performance data about other people on the public internet, and they get no say.

Redaction does not solve it. The shortfall table is keyed to initials, and the initials sit directly
under the first names in the table above. Redacting one and keeping the other re-identifies both.
Redacting enough to be safe leaves a document with one column and no context, which proves nothing
and looks like it is hiding something — because it is.

**And the site does not need it.** [[Brad_Friis_Canwest_Media_Reference_Letter]] already carries the
same fact, signed, named, and in a manager's own words: *"Monthly KPI targets were always achieved
and Brad's dedication to his role resulted in consistent growth of his client base and overall
billing for each quarter."* That is better evidence than the spreadsheet by every measure that
matters — it is attributable, it is consented-in-principle, it is legible, and it exposes nobody.

The cost of withholding is one photograph. The cost of publishing is a page that argues Brad is
trusted, warm and safe to work with, and demonstrates in the same viewport that he will put six
former colleagues' figures online twenty years later. A reader will feel that contradiction without
being able to name it, and it will cost more than the image earns.

**If Brad still wants a number from this period on `/record`,** the defensible form is:
*"The only one of seven on the sales team to make budget that month."*
No image, no percentages, no names, and it is true.
