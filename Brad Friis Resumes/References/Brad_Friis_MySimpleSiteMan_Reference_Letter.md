## The conflict of interest, stated plainly

**The referee shares Brad's surname.** The signature reads TJFriis. Nothing on the document discloses
a relationship, and nothing on the document rules one out either — but a reader does not need it
spelled out. Two Friises, one signing a reference for the other, on a five-person company's
letterhead, is a thing a hiring manager sees in under a second.

This is the highest-risk item in the archive, and the risk is not confined to the document itself.
The letters page works by accumulation: several independent people, no shared incentive, saying the
same things. One reference from an apparent relative does not merely fail to add to that — it invites
the reader to re-examine the other six for the same problem. The cost of publishing it is not the
loss of one document's credibility. It is a discount applied to all of them, applied silently, by a
reader who will never say why.

Against that, what the document actually adds is thin. Its strongest content — cold calling, high
strike rate, client relationships carried in from a previous role — is claimed more credibly by
[[Brad_Friis_Adplus_Advertising_Reference_Letter]] and
[[Brad_Friis_Canwest_Media_Reference_Letter]], both from unrelated signatories on dated letterhead.
There is nothing here that is only here.

**Recommendation: withhold from `/letters`.** The employment itself is a real part of the record and
belongs on `/record` as a line in the career narrative — 2006 onward, web development and software
research, then sole salesperson — stated without the letter behind it. If Brad wants the letter used
at all, the only honest form is publication with the relationship disclosed in the caption, and that
caption then becomes the most memorable sentence on the page. That is the trade, and it is his call.
