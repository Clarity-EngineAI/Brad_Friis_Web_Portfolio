## 3. The moments

Four full-width narrative bands. The first three are the habit working; the fourth is the one time
it didn't, styled quieter — no growth figure in its tag, no resolution beat.

### Moment 1 — 108 calls, zero lost (2020)

> When the lockdowns hit in 2020, I didn't send an email blast to the account base and wait to see
> who called back. I picked up the phone, and then the camera — 108 one-on-one, face-to-face Zoom
> calls, one client at a time, across the whole customer base. Not a check-in. I wanted to hear
> directly what each school actually needed, because nobody had done this before and nobody could
> tell me from a deck. One teacher started her call openly against the software — she didn't want
> it, didn't trust it, didn't see the point. By the end of that same call, something had clicked:
> she'd seen what the tool could actually do, not what she'd assumed it did. She went on to become
> one of the strongest advocates for it in her whole school. I didn't lose a single customer
> through that period. Not because the product sold itself while everyone was stuck at home —
> because I spent the lockdown finding out, one call at a time, what would actually turn a sceptic
> into an advocate.

**Tag:** Lockdown, 2020 · 108 client calls · zero lost

### Moment 2 — the five-campus save, told as what it actually was (2018–2019)

> An education account across five campuses in Australia was seriously weighing a move off the
> platform. The usual response to that is a phone call and a discount. I got on a plane instead. I
> spent a week on the ground, campus by campus, sitting with the leadership team at each one and
> watching how the platform was actually being used — not how it was supposed to be used. What I
> found wasn't the same problem five times; it was five different versions of the same gap between
> what the platform could do and what each campus's staff actually knew how to do with it. I took
> that back and built a strategy out of what I'd learned on the ground: segregating each campus
> inside the one platform, so senior leadership could finally see metrics across the whole
> organisation in one place, and opening up communication and resource-sharing between teachers
> who'd never had a way to work across campuses before. Then I went back a second time to deliver
> it and train the people who'd be running it day to day. The account didn't just stay. It expanded
> to a sixth campus, and started buying two things it had never bought from us before — a paid
> consultancy engagement and an annual managed services agreement.

**Tag:** Multi-campus account, 2018–2019 · retained mid-defection, then expanded

### Moment 3 — 2000AD: selling the sunrise before it existed (1999)

> Before any of the accounts, before the industry, there was an idea that didn't exist yet. 2000AD
> was the largest private millennium event in New Zealand — New Year's Eve 1999 into the year 2000
> — with a full New Zealand artist line-up, a dedicated band stage and a dance marquee, and a
> broadcast that reached the BBC. I worked with TVNZ to sell the whole event on a single idea: New
> Zealand would be first in the world to see the new millennium's sunrise, and this was where to
> watch it happen. I was nineteen, selling sponsorship for a show that hadn't been built, a stage
> that hadn't been booked, a moment that hadn't happened yet. Dominion Breweries and Technics were
> the first two to say yes — not to the idea in the abstract, but to something specific inside it:
> pouring rights across the whole event, and ten seconds of their logo lit up in full colour by the
> dance marquee's laser rig, at midnight, as the year turned. The line-up ended up including The
> Exponents, The New Loungehead, Bongmaster, DJ Lemon, Mark de Clive-Lowe, Andy Vann, Matt Drake and
> Manuel Bundy, among other well-known New Zealand musicians. All these years later, the habit
> hasn't changed: find out what's actually true about the moment in front of you, then sell what
> that moment is actually worth.

**Tag:** 2000AD, NYE 1999 · largest private millennium event in NZ · sold before it existed

### Moment 4 — the one that didn't work (MySimpleSiteMan, 2006–2009)

*Styled distinctly — quieter typography, muted or reversed colour treatment, no growth stat in the
tag.*

> Between Canwest and Xplore, I went out on my own. I founded and ran MySimpleSiteMan, a self-serve
> website builder for small New Zealand businesses — $399 a year, build and manage your own site.
> Over three years, more than $400,000 went into building and maintaining it. The mistake wasn't
> the price or the market — it was assuming that a small business owner who could log in and drag a
> few blocks around actually knew how to build something that turned visitors into customers. The
> system only really supported three-to-six-page brochure sites, with nowhere to grow from there.
> We tried pivoting into a full-service model — in-house copywriters and designers doing the work
> the customers couldn't — but the software had never been built for that either, and eventually I
> let the whole thing go. It's the one time in this career I got the fundamentals wrong, and it's
> also where I learned the thing every account since has depended on: knowing how to use a tool is
> not the same as the tool actually working for you. That's the account-management approach I've
> run ever since.

**Tag:** MySimpleSiteMan, 2006–2009 · founder · $400,000+ invested · closed down

**Business development, Adplus Advertising (later Tracta) · 2001–2003**

two-tier directory product I coordinated, not originated. Understanding what an advertiser actually
needed from a listing, not just what the rate card offered, grew total ad revenue around 60 per
cent and advertiser volume around 40 per cent over three years, with average deal size up around 14
per cent as digital packages started selling alongside print. **[R]**

**Sales, Canwest Media (Radioworks) · 2003–2006**
Harvey Norman was the national account I ran for three years, up against the country's most
aggressive network buyers — joint-promotion campaigns, live-to-air stunts, and one week where I
placed 1,500 ten-second ads at the top of the news and straight after the weather. I held the
account for the full tenure, and don't recall losing one. Met or beat the company's 10 per cent
minimum in each of my three years there, ranging from 10 to 20 per cent, with a best year at 178
per cent of budget. **[R]**

**Business development, Xplore · 2009–2013**
I ran the whole account cycle myself — prospecting, interviews, writing the strategy into the

**National sales and marketing, 99 Corporation · 2013–2015**
Support had always been free; I turned it into a structured, paid line billed a year in advance,
plus a new 50 per cent upfront deposit, setup costs and volume pricing — introduced without
pushback, in the owner's own words "a fair call." I held Xero, LexisNexis, Nielsen and Promax as
national accounts through it. Customers grew around 70 per cent and income around 120 per cent by
my own count — independently corroborated by the owner as around 30 per cent more profit per
customer, and a deposit change that, in his words, made "a massive difference to cashflow and
operations." I left partly because outbound cold-calling didn't suit me, and moved from there into
consultative work — which is where I've stayed for the decade since. **[R]**

**Sales & Marketing Manager, then General Manager · 2015–2025**
The 108 calls and the five-campus save both happened here — told in full above. Off the back of
them: an eight-year run with one college that started with meeting the principal at a conference
and ended with the entire staff onboarded inside a single term; a teacher network that peaked

negotiation that brought it back a year later on my terms — buy-as-needed instead of upfront,
schools invoiced directly in their own currency, and the vendor's usual minimum licence count
