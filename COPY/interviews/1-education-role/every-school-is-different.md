# Every school is different — solutions mapped to culture and character

**In master resume:** **no.** The resume says "running hands-on and remote workshops for non-technical
staff across the customer base" — one programme, delivered across customers. **Brad has now said the
opposite: there was no single programme, because there could not be.**
**Source:** Brad, spoken, 13 August 2026.

---

## Key points

> "Every school had its own nuances and its own set of challenges that needed to be worked out before a
> training and onboarding solution could be delivered."

> "I became really good at providing solutions that mapped to the culture and character of the school."

**This is the sixth instance of the same method, and it is the one that states the method as a
principle rather than demonstrating it.** The other five were occasions. This is Brad naming what the
occasions had in common.

**Three things it establishes that nothing else this session has:**

1. **The onboarding programme was not a programme.** It was a per-customer diagnosis followed by a
   built response. That is a far stronger commercial claim than "ran workshops", and it is *harder*,
   which is why most vendors do not do it.
2. **The variables were concrete and technical, not vibes.** Google, Microsoft or Apple; the school's
   curriculum; its pedagogy; its established ways of working. **Brad had to work inside a customer's
   existing stack and existing method**, not replace them.
3. **It generalises off education cleanly.** Strip the word "school" and the sentence is: every
   customer runs a different stack and a different culture, and the onboarding has to be built for
   theirs. **That is enterprise account management stated in one line**, and it does not fence him
   into edtech.

## The detail

### What varied between customers

Brad named four axes:

| Axis | As he put it |
| --- | --- |
| Platform ecosystem | "They use Google or they use Microsoft or they use Apple" |
| Curriculum | "they have this curriculum" |
| Pedagogy | "…and pedagogy that they follow" |
| Established practice | "they do things this way and that way" |

**The first is the one to keep.** Google, Microsoft and Apple are three named, checkable, recognisable
ecosystems, and a reader outside education immediately understands what it means to build an
onboarding that works differently on each. The other three are education-specific and read as jargon
outside the sector.

### The sequence

> "…needed to be worked out **before** a training and onboarding solution could be delivered."

**Diagnosis precedes delivery. Again.** This is now the third time the same ordering has appeared in
this session, each time in a different setting:

| Setting | The diagnosis-first move |
| --- | --- |
| The five-campus save | A week across the campuses before a strategy was written |
| The principal think tanks | Convened to understand the landscape, with no product in the sentence |
| **Onboarding, every account** | **The school's nuances worked out before a solution was delivered** |

Plus the two where the product was subordinated to the customer's own problem — pedagogy-topic
workshops, and a course library built on the platform rather than about it. **Six instances. It is not
a temperament and it is not five coincidences; it is how he works, stated by him and demonstrated in
five separate settings.**

### The phrase that matters

> "solutions that mapped to the culture and character of the school"

**Compare Adrian Pilgrim's published letter:** *"custom strategies and workarounds that fit our
operations."*

**Brad and his customer independently described the same capability, in near-identical construction,
years apart and without reference to each other.** Brad: solutions mapped to the culture and character.
Pilgrim: strategies that fit our operations.

**This is the second such corroboration found today** — the first being the week in Queensland matching
Pilgrim's same sentence. **The site's most defensible structure is a claim Brad makes and a customer's
letter that independently confirms it, and this pairing is now double-corroborated.**

## The self-assessment problem, and why this one is recoverable

> "I became really good at providing solutions that mapped to the culture and character of the school."

**"I became really good at" is the banned class** — unfalsifiable self-assessment, §7,
`design/01-positioning-brief.md`. Same category as "hugely successful", "performing outstandingly" and
"brilliant branding", all recorded as unpublishable earlier today.

**But the object of the sentence is not the problem — only the framing is.** "Solutions that mapped to
the culture and character of the school" is a description of work, and it is a good one. Strip the
self-praise and the claim survives intact:

- **Unpublishable:** *I became really good at providing solutions that…*
- **Publishable:** *Every school ran a different stack and a different culture, so the onboarding was
  built per school rather than delivered from a template.* Then Pilgrim's letter says whether he was
  good at it.

**This is exactly the §7 rule working as designed: let the customers do the praising.** Brad supplies
the fact; the letter supplies the verdict. Phase 3 has both.

## What this changes about the resume

**Master resume line 24 undersells the work and mis-describes it.** "Built the onboarding, training and
adoption programme, running hands-on and remote workshops for non-technical staff across the customer
base" describes a standardised programme rolled out across customers. Brad has now said the actual work
was the opposite: per-customer diagnosis, then a built response.

**The resume version is both weaker and less true.** Flagged for Phase 3, and worth Brad's attention on
the CV itself — this is a case where the more accurate sentence is also the more impressive one.

---

## Open questions

1. **One school where the stack forced a genuinely different approach.** A Google school and an Apple
   school, and what actually differed between the two onboardings. **This is the concrete instance the
   claim needs, and it is the highest-value follow-up on the page.**
2. **How many schools were onboarded across the decade?** A count would turn "every school was
   different" from a description into a scale claim. The licensed base went 2,800 → 24,500, but that is
   users, not institutions.
3. **Was any of this written down?** A discovery template, a checklist, a per-school plan. If a
   repeatable process was ever documented, it is the difference between craft and method — and it is a
   portfolio artefact.
4. **Did any school choose the platform *because* of this?** If the per-school approach ever won a deal
   against a competitor offering a standard rollout, that is a competitive-win story and the site has
   none.
