# The SIS integration — a product built to remove a customer's worst job

**In master resume:** no. **On the site:** no. **In the brief:** no. **On any document in the project:**
no.
**Source:** Brad, spoken, 13 August 2026, volunteered unprompted.

**The fourth revenue line recorded this session**, and **the first one Brad introduced by describing the
customer's problem instead of the commercial mechanism.** That difference matters — see below.

---

## Key points

**The problem.** Every school must report student achievement and attendance to the government through a
Student Information System. Schools were **exporting spreadsheets from the SIS and manually loading
them into the LMS** to set up their classes and students each year. Brad: *"taking a considerable
amount of time, and was very, very detailed and painful."*

**What was built.** An integration that **pulled the data from the SIS directly into the LMS**, then
**refreshed every record on a 24-hour cycle.**

**The outcome, in Brad's words:** it took *"this entire job off the school's hands"* and gave back
**time and data accuracy.**

**The commercial result:** charged for as a service, **around $30,000 per annum.**

### Why this is a different class of claim from the other three

The other revenue lines this session were commercial repackaging: unbundling support that already
existed (area 3), repricing a licence book (area 5), acquiring an accreditation to reach public funding
(the facilitator work). **This one required building something that did not exist.**

| Revenue line | What it required |
| --- | --- |
| Managed services | Identifying unpriced work and packaging it |
| Annual repricing | Holding value through negotiation |
| Facilitator accreditation | A partnership and a qualification |
| **SIS integration** | **Building a product** |

**"We built an integration in my business"** — this is engineering delivery inside a sales role, sold
as a recurring service. It is the only instance in the archive where Brad's answer to a customer problem
was to have software made.

### The strategic reading, which Brad did not make

**The integration attacks the same structural weakness the facilitator accreditation attacked, from the
other side.**

Brad has already established that schools were lost on overall cost against government-subsidised
Google Classroom and Microsoft 365. **A free product that requires a painful annual data migration is
not actually free** — it costs the school administrator weeks. By removing that job from his own
platform, Brad changed what the school was comparing:

- **Before:** a paid LMS versus a subsidised one, both requiring the same painful setup.
- **After:** a paid LMS where the job disappears, versus a subsidised one where it does not.

**Three of the four revenue lines are answers to the same competitive problem** — the accreditation
routed government money to his training, the integration removed a cost the competitor still imposed,
and both made the price gap less decisive. **Whether Brad built them as a coherent strategy or arrived
at them separately is unknown and must be asked, not assumed.** See open question 5.

### The retention reading, which is the one for the site

**A school that has handed its entire SIS-to-LMS data pipeline to a vendor, on a daily refresh, is not
casually switching platforms.** Unlike the five-campus linking strategy — where A19 ruled out the
lock-in reading — this genuinely is a switching cost, and an earned one: the customer chose to hand over
the job because the job was painful.

**But it publishes as value, not as lock-in**, for the same reason recorded at
`area-1-five-campus-save.md`: *he made the product worth more to them* survives a hiring manager's
scrutiny, and *he made them hard to leave* does not. **The mechanism is identical; the framing is not
optional.**

## The detail

> "Another area that I generated income was through an integration with the student information system
> that all schools have to use to report student achievement and attendance to the government. At the
> time, schools were in a position of using spreadsheets exported from the student information system
> into the learning management system I was selling, to set up their classes and students for the year.
> This task was taking a considerable amount of time, and was very, very detailed and painful. We built
> an integration in my business that would take the information from the student information system and
> import it directly into the learning management system. And then on a twenty-four-hour cycle, update
> all the records daily — taking this entire job off the school's hands and giving them back significant
> amount of time and accuracy with their data records. We charged for the service, also generating
> around thirty thousand dollars per annum."

### The pronoun — "we built", "in my business"

**Fourth occurrence of the pattern this session, and this one is genuinely mixed.** "**We** built an
integration in **my** business." The build is plural; the business is possessive.

**"My business" is not literal and must not be published as such.** Brad was an employee of the
education employer throughout this decade — the phrase is colloquial for *the business I was in*.
**Any copy that renders this as Brad owning a business would be a factual error**, and it would collide
with the never-name-the-employer rule in a way that invites exactly the wrong question.

**What is safely claimable:** Brad identified the problem and the integration was built to solve it.
**What is not yet established:** whether he specified it, commissioned it, sold it, or built it. See
open question 1. **The register bars the origination claim until he answers.**

## The figure — what may and may not be done with it

**$30,000 per annum, alongside the managed services vertical's $40,000–$50,000.**

**Do not add them.** A "$70–80k of recurring revenue created" sentence is arithmetically available and
**barred**, for three reasons: the two lines may have overlapped in time or in customers; neither
figure's period is established; and Brad has not made the combined claim himself. **The project has
already disclaimed two composite figures** (the 400 per cent, the 130 per cent). **A third would be
the same error committed knowingly.**

**Do not compute lifetime value.** Same bar as area 3.

**"Around" is Brad's hedge and survives verbatim.** Not "$30,000". Not "over $30,000".

revenue figure**: it says the product solved a problem every customer actually had.

**Scope discipline.** Brad said *NZ schools*. The book also covered Australia. **Never widen this to
"every school" or "the whole book"** — the claim is New Zealand and stays New Zealand until Brad
rules on the Australian side.

**Do not multiply it by the $30k.** Same bar as everywhere else.

**Pairs with the 90 per cent support-agreement attach rate** (`area-3-managed-services.md`). Two
independent products, two near-total attach rates, across the same book. **That pattern is itself the
claim** — and it argues land-keep-grow better than either figure alone. Flagged for Phase 3 as a
connection to test, not yet a written line.

## Open questions

1. **Who built it, and whose idea was it?** "We built an integration in my business" is ambiguous on
   both counts. **Did Brad specify it, commission it, sell it, or build it?** **Highest-value question
   here** — it is the difference between *he sold an integration* and *he saw a customer problem and
   had a product built to solve it*. The second is a materially rarer claim.
2. **How many schools bought it**, out of how many in the book? Attach rate. **Third time this question
   has gone unasked across three revenue lines.**
3. **When was it built?** Position in the decade unknown.
4. **Was it built for one school and productised, or built as a product from the start?** The first is
   a better story and a more common origin.
5. **Was the sequence deliberate?** Integration, accreditation and managed services all reduce the
   effective cost gap against subsidised competitors. **Ask whether Brad saw them as one strategy or
   arrived at them separately.** Do not assume coherence that was not there — but if it was deliberate,
   it is the strongest single argument in the archive.
6. **Which SIS?** New Zealand schools of this period predominantly used KAMAR or eTAP. Naming it makes
   the claim checkable; **confirm rather than assume.**
7. **Does the integration still exist?** **Do not ask.** Adjacent to how the role ended, which is under
   the settlement gag.
