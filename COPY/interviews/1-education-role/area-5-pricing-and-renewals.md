# Capability area 5 — pricing, licensing and renewals

**In master resume:** not as pricing. The decade's commercial terms have never been described anywhere
in the project except as "multi-year licensing" in the capability area list.
**On the site:** nothing. **This is the first pricing evidence the project has ever had.**
**Source:** Brad, spoken, 13 August 2026, answering a question about the managed services agreement at
the five-campus account. **The answer went somewhere better than the question.**

---

## Key points

**Brad raised the per-student licence price every year, across every institution in the book, for the
duration of the role.** Starting point around **$10 per student**; at his departure, around **$23 per
student**.

**This is the first evidence in the entire archive that Brad priced rather than sold.** Every other
claim in the project is about winning accounts, keeping accounts or growing usage. This is about what
the accounts paid, and it moved in one direction for ten years.

**The commercial reading, which Brad did not make.** The impressive fact is not the size of the
increase. It is that **the increases happened annually, across the whole book, while the same accounts
were renewing** — Sancta Maria eight times over. Raising price once is a decision. Raising it every
year for a decade without losing the accounts is a capability, and it is the exact thing a retention
seat is paid to do. **The price track and the renewal track corroborate each other and neither was
offered as evidence for the other.**

**Brad raised the confidentiality problem himself, unprompted, and was right to.** See the ruling
below.

## The detail

> "Across all of the institutions that I worked with, I lifted their per student license price each
> year. So most schools started around ten dollars. And when I left, we were sitting at about
> twenty-three dollars per student. I'm not sure if we should be publicly claiming that, because
> obviously what each school pays is confidential, and different schools were paying different prices.
> So I'm not sure how we handle that. Maybe we use a percentage."

### What the numbers actually say

| Reading | Figure | Verdict |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Total increase | **130 per cent** | **Do not publish.** See below |
| Compound annual rate, if over ~10 years | **~9 per cent a year** | **The only figure worth considering**, and only once the period is confirmed |
| Start and end points | $10 → $23 | **Do not publish.** Customer-identifying |

**Why 130 per cent must not be published, even though it is arithmetically true.** It is the same shape
as the 400 per cent Brad disclaimed earlier the same day: a large percentage on a small base over an
unstated period. A hiring manager reads "130 per cent price increase" as either an inflated claim or a
vendor squeezing schools. **Neither is what happened, and both readings are worse than publishing
nothing.** The number is true and still damaging — this is the distinction the register's Tier C exists
to hold.

**Why the annual rate is the better unit.** Around 9 per cent a year sounds unremarkable, which is
precisely its value: it is credible on sight, it survives a reference check, and **the claim it
supports is durability rather than magnitude.** The strong sentence is not *he raised prices a lot*.
It is *he raised them every year and the customers stayed*.

**The period is not yet confirmed and everything depends on it.** ~9 per cent assumes the full decade.
Over five years the same endpoints are ~18 per cent a year, which is a materially different claim.
**Nothing publishes until Brad confirms the span.** See open question 1.

### The confidentiality ruling

**Brad's own instinct, recorded verbatim above, and it governs.** Adopted as a standing rule for this
area:

- **Never publish $10, $23, or any per-student dollar figure.** What a school pays is confidential, and
  Brad has said so.
- **Never publish the range**, which lets a reader reconstruct individual pricing.
- **Never publish 130 per cent.** True, customer-adjacent, and it invites the worst available reading.
- **"Most schools started around ten"** — the hedge is Brad's and it is load-bearing. Different schools
  paid different prices. Any published version must not imply a single price applied across the book.
- **If a figure publishes at all, it is the annual rate**, and only after the period is confirmed.

**Recommended published form, pending the two open questions:** the mechanism and the durability, no
dollars — *held accounts through annual per-student price increases across the decade, with schools
renewing throughout*. This is publishable today except for the authority question below.

## The question that decides what this claim is

**Was the annual increase Brad's decision, or the vendor's list price?** Not yet asked. The two answers
produce two different and non-interchangeable claims:

| If | The honest claim is | Class of evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The vendor set list price annually and Brad implemented it | He **held a book of accounts through a decade of annual price increases** | **Retention under commercial pressure.** Strong, and it corroborates the renewal record |
| Brad set or negotiated the increases | He **priced the book upward every year** | **Pricing authority.** Rarer, and materially stronger for the target role |

**Do not guess and do not let Phase 3 guess.** Brad's phrasing — "I lifted their per student license
price each year" — reads as the second, but "we were sitting at about twenty-three" reads as the
company. **The pronoun shift is exactly the pattern flagged over the Australian territory award**, where
"we" and "us" marked a company achievement that copy must not convert into a personal one.

## Did anyone leave over price? — answered 13 August 2026

**Asked because the claim needed protecting, not building. The answer is better than a clean sweep.**

> "I never lost a school based on a price increase on a specific year. However, we did lose the
> occasional school based on overall cost. Google Classroom and Microsoft Office 365 are subsidised by
> the New Zealand government, and Schoology is quite expensive by comparison."

*Transcription note: given by voice and partly garbled in the opening clause. Read as above; the two
halves are unambiguous and were stated in this order. **Confirm exact wording before publication.***

**Two facts, and the concession is what makes the win publishable.**

| | Claim |
| --- | --- |
| **Never** | Lost a school because of that year's price increase |
| **Did** | Lose the occasional school on overall cost |

**Why the distinction is real and not a hedge.** These are different commercial events. A school
leaving over an annual increase is a failure of account management — the relationship did not carry the
rise. A school leaving because a government-subsidised competitor is structurally cheaper is a market
condition no account manager can price against. **Brad bounded his own claim to the thing he
controlled, unprompted.**

**This is the Tier C pattern running in reverse.** All session, Brad's instinct has been to
characterise himself generously. Here he volunteered a loss against a precise claim. **A candidate who
says "I never lost an account" is not believed. A candidate who says "never on a price rise — though we
did lose some on overall cost against subsidised competitors" is believed about both halves.** The
concession is the credibility.

### The competitive frame — new to the project, and it reframes everything above it

**The decade was spent selling a paid platform against free-at-point-of-use, government-subsidised
alternatives.** Google Classroom and Microsoft 365, subsidised by the New Zealand government, with
Schoology expensive by comparison. **Nothing in the archive has ever said this.**

It changes the reading of every retention claim on the site. The eight renewals, the five-campus save,
the annual price rises — **all of it happened while the customer had a heavily subsidised alternative
sitting there.** Retention against a free competitor is a different order of claim from retention in a
normal market. **This is the frame that makes the pricing material impressive rather than routine**, and
it is the strongest argument yet for why the per-student increases are worth publishing at all.

**Publishable form — the concession travels with the claim.** Never publish "never lost a school over a
price increase" alone. Stripped of its bound it becomes the absolute class already ruled against at C2
("no school has ever been remotely close"). Both halves, or neither.

2. **Whose decision were the increases** — Brad's, negotiated, or the vendor's list price applied? See
   the table above. **Determines whether this is pricing authority or retention under pressure.**
3. ~~**Did any account leave over a price increase?**~~ **Answered 13 August 2026.** Never on an
   increase; the occasional school on overall cost, against government-subsidised competitors.
4. **Roughly how many schools were lost on overall cost, out of how many in the book?** "Occasional" is
   Brad's word and survives regardless. A rough proportion would let the retention claims carry a
   magnitude. **Do not press if it is not to hand** — the bounded form already publishes.
5. **Confirm the transcription** of the opening clause, which came through garbled by voice.

   worth more than the entire pricing section.

   paying different prices" suggests the latter, which would be stronger evidence again.
8. **Is there a retrieval route** — a price list, a rate card, a renewal contract? Would move this from
