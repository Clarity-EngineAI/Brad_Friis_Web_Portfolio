# Ecommerce + AI Personalisation Editorial Framework

## 1. What the content programme should achieve

The blog should not become a news feed about whatever AI feature was announced this week.

Its job is to build a body of useful material around one larger idea:

> **How ecommerce businesses can use AI to make finding, evaluating and buying products easier for customers.**

That gives the content a commercial centre while still leaving room to cover search, recommendations, product data, conversational shopping, agents, merchandising, conversion, privacy and emerging technology.

The articles should collectively position the author as someone who understands:

- what the technology actually does;
- where it creates commercial value;
- where the hype gets ahead of reality;
- what ecommerce businesses need before implementing it;
- how different AI technologies fit together;
- what a sensible implementation looks like;
- what businesses can do next.

The reader should regularly finish an article thinking:

**“I understand this better now, and I can see how it might apply to our store.”**

Not:

**“Apparently AI is transforming ecommerce. Again.”**

These pillars give the blog enough breadth to cover the sector without becoming random.

# 12. Every article should use one of five editorial angles

The pillars define **what we talk about**.

These define **how we talk about it**.

### A. Explain

> What is semantic search?

Useful, but should never become Wikipedia with nicer typography.

### B. Diagnose

> Why customers can't find products on your ecommerce site

Often stronger because the reader recognises an existing problem.

### C. Challenge

> More personalisation data doesn't necessarily create better personalisation

Use contrarian arguments only when defensible.

### D. Apply

> Five things to fix before implementing AI search

Makes expertise useful.

### E. Explore

> What happens when AI agents become ecommerce customers?

Useful for frontier developments where conclusions are still emerging.

A healthy content programme should deliberately rotate these.

# 13. Standard article structure

Articles should not all look identical, but every article should contain these functional elements.

## 1. Recognition

Start with something the reader has probably experienced.

Not:

> Artificial intelligence is rapidly transforming the ecommerce industry.

Instead:

> Your customer searches for “a jacket I can wear to work when it's raining”.

> Your catalogue contains “UrbanShell WX-240”.

> Everybody involved believes they've explained themselves perfectly.

That immediately creates the problem.

---

## 2. The governing idea

Make the article's central argument clear early.

One article.

One primary idea.

Subtopics should support it rather than becoming separate miniature articles.

---

## 3. Why it matters commercially

Connect the technology to:

- customer effort;
- conversion;
- product discovery;
- purchase confidence;
- returns;
- basket size;
- time;
- operational efficiency;
- margin.

Never invent benefits.

Explain the mechanism.

---

## 4. How it works

Give enough technical explanation to make the reader genuinely smarter.

Avoid both extremes:

**Too shallow:**  
“AI analyses customer data.”

**Too technical:**  
A 900-word detour into transformer architecture.

---

## 5. Where it works

Show realistic ecommerce applications.

---

## 6. Where it doesn't

Every serious article should acknowledge limitations.

This builds considerably more credibility than treating every AI feature as compulsory.

---

## 7. Practical implications

Answer:

> What should an ecommerce business do differently because of this?

---

# 14. Mandatory visual framework

Every substantial article should contain **at least three meaningful visuals**.

Images should explain something, not simply decorate blank spaces.

## Visual 1: Hero image

Purpose:

**Create curiosity and communicate the main idea.**

Preferred style:

- refined editorial illustration;
- strong visual metaphor;
- ecommerce context;
- minimal text;
- designed consistently across the blog.

Example:

Article:

**What happens when your next customer isn't human?**

Hero image:

A shopper standing behind an AI agent while the agent examines several digital storefronts on their behalf.

---

## Visual 2: Explanation graphic

Purpose:

**Make the mechanism understandable.**

Good formats:

- process diagrams;
- customer journeys;
- before/after diagrams;
- architecture diagrams;
- decision trees;
- funnels;
- flow charts.

Example:

`Shopper intent`
  
`-> conversational AI`
  
`-> catalogue understanding`
  
`-> product filtering`
  
`-> ranked shortlist`
  
`-> purchase decision`

---

## Visual 3: Applied example

Purpose:

**Help the reader imagine the technology on an actual ecommerce website.**

Use:

- ecommerce UI mock-ups;
- annotated product grids;
- search examples;
- recommendation interfaces;
- AI shopping assistant interfaces;
- before/after screenshots.

This is probably the most commercially valuable visual because it changes AI from an abstract concept into:

> “I can imagine this on our website.”

---

## Optional Visual 4: Evidence

Use when appropriate:

- charts;
- tables;
- benchmark data;
- research findings;
- experiment results.

Never manufacture statistics simply because a graph would look nice.

# 16. Mandatory article ending

Every article should finish with three sections.

## Summary

No generic conclusion.

Use:

### In short

Three to five bullets capturing the important ideas.

For example:

- AI personalisation is increasingly driven by immediate shopping intent rather than historical profiles alone.
- Semantic product understanding allows ecommerce systems to respond to meaning rather than exact keywords.
- Better AI usually requires better product data.
- The commercial goal is not “more AI”. It is helping customers reach a suitable product with less effort.

---

# 17. Actionable next steps

Every article must tell the reader what to do.

Use:

### What you can do now

Usually **3-5 practical actions**.

For example:

1. Review your 50 most common internal search queries.
2. Identify queries that return poor or irrelevant products.
3. Compare the language customers use with the attributes stored in your catalogue.
4. Investigate whether your ecommerce platform supports semantic or hybrid search.
5. Establish a baseline for search exits and search-assisted conversion before changing anything.

These should be specific enough to actually undertake.

Avoid:

> “Embrace AI.”

Nobody has ever finished a productive Wednesday afternoon by adding that to the task list.

# 18. The standard soft commercial close

The call to action should feel like the natural final sentence of a useful article.

Never:

> BOOK A FREE AI STRATEGY CALL TODAY!

Never:

> Transform your ecommerce business with cutting-edge AI solutions.

Instead use a structure such as:

### Want to explore what this could look like on your store?

If you're looking at **[TOPIC / FEATURE]** for your own ecommerce website, feel free to get in touch. I'm happy to talk through how it could work with your catalogue, customers and existing ecommerce setup - including whether it actually makes sense for your business.

No hard sell. Just a practical conversation about what would be involved.

If **[FEATURE]** is something you've been considering for your ecommerce site, I'm always happy to compare notes. Get in touch and we can look at what a practical implementation could look like for your store.

# 19. The complete article formula

For future articles, use this as the working brief:

**ARTICLE TOPIC**

One specific ecommerce + AI issue.

**READER**

NZ/Australian ecommerce owner, manager, marketer or digital lead.

**GOVERNING IDEA**

One sentence explaining what the article is really arguing.

**WHY THE READER CARES**

Commercial/customer consequence.

**INFORMATION GAIN**

Something more useful than generic internet consensus.

**STRUCTURE**

Recognition  
-> problem/tension  
-> explanation  
-> mechanism  
-> practical ecommerce example  
-> limitations  
-> implications

**VISUAL 1**

Hero editorial illustration.

**VISUAL 2**

Mechanism/explanatory graphic.

**VISUAL 3**

Applied ecommerce interface.

**ENDING**

### In short

3-5 useful conclusions.

### What you can do now

3-5 actions.

### Want to explore this on your own store?

A low-pressure invitation to discuss implementing the specific feature covered in the article.

# 20. Recommended content mix

To keep the blog authoritative rather than overly promotional or futurist:

| Content type | Approximate share |
|---|---:|
| Practical/application | 30% |
| Problem/diagnostic | 25% |
| Technology explainers | 20% |
| Contrarian/opinion | 15% |
| Emerging/future | 10% |

That balance matters.

If half the site discusses what ecommerce might look like in 2030, readers may find it interesting but have very little to do on Monday morning.

The strongest positioning comes from connecting the frontier to the practical:

**Here is what is changing.**

**Here is why it matters.**

**Here is what is real.**

**Here is what isn't yet.**

**And here is what you can actually do about it.**
