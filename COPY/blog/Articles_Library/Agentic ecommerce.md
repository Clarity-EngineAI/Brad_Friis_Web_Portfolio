# Your Customer Hasn't Changed. What They Need Has.

## Why the next generation of ecommerce personalisation is moving from customer profiles to shopping intent

An ecommerce store has known Sarah for three years.

She buys running gear. Usually practical stuff. Mid-range prices. Women's size 10. Mostly black. Rarely anything that could reasonably be described as glamorous.

So the personalisation engine has her sorted.

Until Sarah needs something to wear to a wedding.

Suddenly, three years of meticulously collected evidence about running shorts is considerably less helpful.

Sarah hasn't changed.

**What she is trying to achieve has.**

That distinction sits at the centre of one of the more useful developments happening in ecommerce personalisation.

For years, personalisation has concentrated heavily on answering:

**Who is this customer?**

Increasingly, AI allows ecommerce businesses to ask a different question:

**What is this customer trying to do right now?**

And for many purchasing decisions, the second question may be considerably more valuable.

## Traditional personalisation has a memory problem

There is nothing inherently wrong with using customer history.

Past purchases, products viewed, brand preferences, price sensitivity and category behaviour can all contain useful signals.

If someone has bought the same coffee beans every six weeks for two years, their history is probably telling you something useful.

The problem starts when we mistake **history for intent**.

Traditional personalisation often relies on relatively persistent characteristics:

- previous purchases;
- customer segments;
- demographic information;
- loyalty status;
- preferred categories;
- products previously viewed;
- inferred interests.

These help build a picture of the customer.

But people don't arrive at ecommerce websites as fixed customer profiles.

They arrive because something happened.

They broke something.

They need a present.

They're going camping.

Their child started school.

They adopted a dog.

They've been invited to a wedding.

They finally accepted that the television held together with electrical tape has probably served its country.

The shopping mission changes.

A personalisation system that continues serving recommendations primarily because of what happened months ago risks being very sophisticated about the wrong thing.

## Intent-based personalisation starts with the current visit

Modern AI recommendation systems can incorporate events occurring during the current shopping session alongside historical information.

Google's current AI Commerce Search documentation, for example, describes recommendation systems that use product views, clicks, add-to-cart behaviour and purchases, with recent user events incorporated into personalised recommendations. Google specifically distinguishes underlying model training from the real-time personalisation layer, which can respond to events that happened only minutes earlier.

Shopify similarly describes current AI personalisation as combining historical information with behaviour occurring in real time rather than relying solely on manually constructed customer segments.

That changes what an ecommerce site can potentially respond to.

Imagine someone enters a large homewares store.

They:

1. search for `outdoor dining`;
2. view three six-seat tables;
3. filter for aluminium;
4. ignore several cheaper timber options;
5. inspect product dimensions;
6. view two weather-resistant chair sets;
7. add one table to their basket.

Individually, none of those actions tells you very much.

Together, they begin to describe a shopping mission.

The customer appears to want an outdoor dining setting, probably for around six people, with durability or low maintenance carrying more weight than simply finding the cheapest option.

The store does not necessarily need to know the customer's age, occupation or what they bought last Christmas to become more useful.

It needs to pay attention.

## This isn't about throwing the customer profile away

That would simply replace one oversimplification with another.

History still matters.

Suppose two customers are currently browsing espresso machines.

One has previously bought premium coffee grinders, specialist beans and accessories.

The other has never purchased coffee equipment from the store.

Their current intent may look similar, but the available context is different.

A good system can use both.

The more useful shift is from thinking:

**“This person belongs to Segment B.”**

towards:

**“Based on what we know and what they are doing now, what seems most relevant at this moment?”**

That is a much more fluid version of personalisation.

It can also accommodate anonymous visitors more effectively.

A first-time visitor doesn't arrive with a lovingly maintained CRM history stretching back to 2019.

But they do start behaving almost immediately.

They search.

Click.

Filter.

Compare.

Backtrack.

Add things.

Remove things.

Ignore things.

Each action potentially provides another clue.

Google's documentation is fairly explicit on this point: its recommendation models can begin incorporating user behaviour immediately, and it recommends sending user events in real time or close to it when effective personalisation is required.

For ecommerce businesses, that creates an interesting possibility:

**Useful personalisation doesn't always need to wait until you know exactly who somebody is.**

## What could actually change on the website?

This is where “AI personalisation” can become annoyingly vague.

A system understanding intent is only commercially useful if the website does something useful with that understanding.

Consider our outdoor furniture shopper.

As their intent becomes clearer, the store might progressively change:

### Product ranking

Outdoor tables meeting the apparent requirements move higher in results.

### Recommendations

Instead of generic “Customers also bought” suggestions, recommendations favour compatible chairs, covers or products suitable for the same use case.

### Category merchandising

Products that appear relevant to the current shopping mission receive greater prominence.

### Content

Relevant buying guidance could become easier to access - for example, information about weather resistance or caring for aluminium furniture.

### Bundles

Suitable combinations might be offered because the products make sense together, not simply because they have historically appeared in the same baskets.

### Navigation

The experience can provide faster routes into the parts of the catalogue the shopper appears to be exploring.

This doesn't mean every pixel needs to rearrange itself whenever somebody moves the mouse.

That sounds less like personalisation and more like trying to shop during a mild earthquake.

The objective is relevance.

Not movement.

## The real opportunity is reducing customer effort

It is tempting to judge personalisation by how impressive the technology looks.

That is probably the wrong test.

The customer does not care that you have implemented a machine-learning recommendation architecture.

They care whether buying a dining table has somehow become less annoying.

That puts a much more useful commercial objective around AI personalisation:

**Reduce the amount of unnecessary work between wanting something and confidently choosing it.**

That work can include:

- repeatedly adjusting filters;
- opening endless browser tabs;
- comparing near-identical products;
- returning to category pages;
- trying different search terms;
- checking compatibility manually;
- scrolling through products that were never plausible candidates.

If personalisation reduces some of that effort while still leaving the customer in control, it is doing something worthwhile.

If it merely produces a personalised banner saying “Welcome back, Sarah”, we may have somewhat overcooked the term artificial intelligence.

## There is also such a thing as over-personalising

Intent is inferred.

That word matters.

Someone viewing three premium products does not prove they have suddenly developed expensive taste.

They may be comparing them with a cheaper model.

A visitor looking repeatedly at a particular product may desperately want it.

Or they may be trying to work out why anybody would pay $1,800 for it.

Behaviour provides evidence.

It does not provide telepathy.

This is why useful personalisation needs room for uncertainty.

The system should improve the odds that relevant products appear without becoming so confident that it removes alternatives the customer still needs.

There is a practical difference between:

**“These appear particularly relevant, so let's move them higher.”**

and:

**“We have determined your destiny. Here are three chairs.”**

The first assists discovery.

The second can damage it.

## The implementation question isn't “How much can we personalise?”

A better question is:

**Where does our current ecommerce experience force customers to do unnecessary work?**

Start there.

If customers struggle to find suitable products, investigate product ranking and discovery.

If people repeatedly compare similar products, look at how intent signals could help surface meaningful differences.

If accessory purchases are difficult, investigate contextual recommendations.

If most customers find exactly what they need through normal navigation, leave it alone.

Not every ecommerce interaction requires AI intervention.

A good implementation should make something measurably easier.

Otherwise, you have mostly installed another thing.

# In short

- Traditional ecommerce personalisation relies heavily on what businesses already know about a customer.
- Intent-based personalisation gives greater weight to what the shopper appears to be trying to accomplish during the current visit.
- Search behaviour, product views, clicks, filters and cart activity can provide useful real-time signals.
- Historical customer data remains valuable, but it should not automatically outweigh current shopping behaviour.
- The objective isn't to personalise everything. It is to reduce the unnecessary effort involved in finding and choosing the right product.
- Intent is still an inference, so good systems should improve relevance without becoming overly restrictive.

# What you can do now

### 1. Choose one important shopping journey

Don't start with the entire website.

Pick a category with:

- plenty of products;
- meaningful differences between them;
- reasonable traffic; and
- evidence that customers have to make choices.

### 2. Map the signals customers already create

Document what you can currently observe during a session:

- searches;
- filters;
- product views;
- comparison activity;
- add-to-cart events;
- removals;
- purchases.

You may already be collecting much of this data without using it to shape the experience.

### 3. Identify where customer intent becomes clearer

Look for sequences rather than isolated clicks.

For example:

`outdoor furniture`

-> `six-seat tables`

-> `aluminium`

-> `three product views`

-> `dimensions checked`

That sequence contains considerably more information than “visitor viewed product 427”.

### 4. Decide what the store could usefully change

Don't begin by asking what the AI can do.

Ask what would help the customer.

Could you improve:

- product ranking?
- recommendations?
- bundles?
- navigation?
- relevant buying information?

Choose one.

### 5. Establish the baseline before implementing anything

Measure the customer journey now.

Depending on the use case, that might include:

- search exits;
- product views before purchase;
- add-to-cart rate;
- conversion;
- recommendation acceptance;
- revenue per session;
- returns.

Without a baseline, it becomes remarkably easy for an AI project to be declared successful because everybody agrees that it looks clever.
