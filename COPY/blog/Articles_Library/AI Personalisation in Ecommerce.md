# AI Personalisation in Ecommerce Is Moving Beyond “People Like You Also Bought”

For years, ecommerce personalisation has mostly meant recognising a customer and rearranging the furniture.

They bought running shoes, so show them running socks. They looked at outdoor furniture, so follow them around the internet with the same outdoor chair for three weeks. They live in Auckland, so perhaps remove the banner advertising free delivery to Brisbane.

Useful, certainly. Cutting edge, not especially.

The more interesting shift happening in ecommerce now is away from simply asking **“Who is this customer?”** and towards understanding **“What is this person trying to accomplish right now?”**

That sounds like a small distinction.

It isn't.

Modern AI systems can combine what someone types, what they view, what they ignore, what is in their basket, previous purchases, product attributes and the behaviour of other shoppers to build a much richer picture of intent.

And increasingly, they can do it during the shopping session rather than waiting for another 20 transactions to work out what someone probably likes.

The result could be the biggest change to ecommerce product discovery since faceted search gave us the privilege of selecting “black”, “medium” and “under $200” ourselves.

## Personalisation is becoming an intent problem

Traditional personalisation relies heavily on customer history.

That works well when history is relevant.

It becomes less useful when someone who normally buys $60 T-shirts suddenly needs an outfit for a winter wedding in Queenstown.

Their customer profile says one thing.

Their current problem says something completely different.

This is where the newer generation of AI commerce software becomes interesting.

AI shopping assistants can use **in-session signals** from anonymous visitors as well as account, purchase and loyalty information from known customers. Shopify, for example, now describes AI personal shoppers that combine browsing behaviour, filters, cart contents, historical data and contextual signals to shape recommendations.

The distinction matters because intent can change far faster than demographics.

A customer doesn't need to become a new “segment” before the store responds differently.

The store can respond to the job they are trying to get done.

## Semantic search is quietly becoming one of the most useful AI upgrades

One of the most commercially practical applications isn't particularly glamorous.

It is search.

Traditional ecommerce search has always suffered from a fairly obvious problem: customers don't necessarily describe products using the words retailers used when building the catalogue.

Someone searches:

**“Shoes for a rainy outdoor wedding.”**

Your product catalogue contains:

**“Women's leather Chelsea boot - black.”**

Keyword search may see very little relationship between those two things.

Semantic search can.

Vector embeddings represent products and queries according to their underlying meaning, allowing systems to identify relationships that literal keyword matching misses.

The strongest systems increasingly use **hybrid search**, combining semantic similarity with traditional keyword matching rather than blindly replacing one with the other. Algolia's current NeuralSearch product, for example, combines vector and keyword search, while Google's AI Commerce Search combines language models, behavioural signals and product catalogue information.

Target has publicly described rebuilding part of its own product-search infrastructure using hybrid keyword and vector search. The company reported a 20% improvement in product-discovery relevance from the work.

For ecommerce operators, this is an important point.

The next leap in personalisation may not begin with generating a unique homepage for every visitor.

It may begin with finally understanding what somebody meant when they typed four slightly unhelpful words into the search box.

## The search box itself is starting to disappear

The next step is conversational product discovery.

Instead of forcing a shopper to translate what they want into filters, the software can interrogate the problem.

Imagine a customer shopping for a coffee machine.

They say:

> “I want something good for flat whites, but I don't want to spend 20 minutes cleaning it every morning.”

A traditional catalogue gives them espresso machines.

A better recommendation engine ranks them.

A conversational commerce system can ask:

**“Would you prefer automatic milk frothing, or are you happy using a steam wand?”**

That answer dramatically changes the relevant products.

Google's current AI commerce technology supports this kind of multi-turn conversational refinement, while newer agentic-commerce platforms from companies such as Algolia are being designed around guided discovery, comparisons and recommendations grounded in actual catalogue data.

This changes the role of personalisation.

Instead of the retailer silently guessing what somebody wants, the system can **learn through conversation**.

That is both more powerful and, in many cases, considerably less creepy.

## Personalisation is also leaving the ecommerce website

This is probably the development retailers should watch most closely.

The personalised shopping experience increasingly starts before someone visits your store.

Customers are asking AI systems to:

- find products matching several requirements;
- compare alternatives;
- explain trade-offs;
- shortlist options;
- check prices and specifications;
- recommend the best choice for a particular situation.

OpenAI's shopping research, for example, allows shoppers to refine product requirements conversationally and adapts recommendations using preferences, constraints and feedback. Its newer product-discovery infrastructure also allows merchants to supply structured catalogue information through the Agentic Commerce Protocol.

Shopify is building similar infrastructure around agentic storefronts and its Universal Commerce Protocol.

This creates an uncomfortable but useful question for ecommerce businesses:

**What happens when your website is no longer where the customer makes most of the decision?**

AI may research 30 products and recommend three.

Your product either makes that shortlist or it doesn't.

By the time the shopper reaches your product page, the traditional awareness-consideration-product-discovery journey may already have happened somewhere else.

Early Shopify data gives some indication of this behaviour. In Q1 2026, Shopify reported that AI-referred visitors landing on product pages converted at nearly 50% higher rates than comparable organic-search visitors, with 14% higher average order values. Shopify rightly describes these signals as early rather than proof of a settled channel.

The implication is more important than the exact percentage.

AI can compress product research before the customer ever arrives.

## Which makes product data surprisingly important

There is a slightly inconvenient truth underneath almost every impressive AI commerce demonstration.

The AI still needs decent information.

A sophisticated recommendation model sitting on top of a catalogue containing titles such as:

**“Premium Series Model X3000”**

with a two-line description and six unexplained technical fields is not suddenly going to develop telepathy.

Good AI personalisation depends heavily on good product information.

That increasingly means capturing attributes beyond the traditional basics:

- intended use;
- compatibility;
- materials;
- dimensions;
- fit;
- environment;
- style;
- constraints;
- product relationships;
- alternatives;
- “best for” scenarios;
- customer questions;
- meaningful differences between similar products.

This data improves traditional filters.

It improves semantic search.

It improves recommendations.

And increasingly it helps external AI systems understand when a product should be recommended at all.

Google explicitly recommends sharing structured ecommerce data so its systems can understand products more accurately, while Shopify's 2026 AI-search analysis similarly points to richer, structured catalogue data as important for AI-mediated discovery.

There is a useful lesson here.

Some businesses shopping for an AI personalisation platform probably need a better product-data model first.

Buying the clever bit before fixing the boring bit remains one of technology's more dependable traditions.

## The most advanced system is not necessarily the one that knows the most about you

There is another direction AI personalisation could take that deserves more attention: **using less customer data, but interpreting it better.**

Historically, increasingly personalised experiences tended to demand increasingly detailed customer profiles.

That equation is becoming less absolute.

A system capable of understanding:

> “I need a lightweight jacket that will handle Auckland rain but won't look like I'm about to climb Everest”

already knows quite a lot about what matters for this purchase.

It doesn't necessarily need three years of browsing history to be useful.

That matters in New Zealand and Australia because AI personalisation still sits inside existing privacy obligations.

New Zealand's Office of the Privacy Commissioner states that the Privacy Act and Information Privacy Principles apply when organisations use AI with personal information and recommends conducting and regularly updating a Privacy Impact Assessment.

Australia's OAIC similarly advises businesses to minimise personal information supplied to AI systems, explain how AI is being used and ensure data is collected and used for appropriate purposes.

Better personalisation should not therefore mean assembling the largest possible dossier on every person who once looked at a barbecue.

Sometimes the smarter system is the one capable of doing more with less.

## Where ecommerce businesses should focus now

For most small and mid-sized retailers, the sensible path isn't to install every AI feature currently capable of surviving a product demonstration.

Start closer to the buying problem.

### 1. Examine failed product discovery

Look at:

- zero-result searches;
- low-converting searches;
- filters shoppers repeatedly combine;
- products viewed together;
- common comparison questions;
- chat and customer-service enquiries.

These reveal the language customers actually use.

### 2. Improve the product catalogue

Give AI something meaningful to reason over.

Add use cases, attributes, compatibility information, meaningful distinctions and natural-language descriptions where they are genuinely useful.

### 3. Test semantic or hybrid search

This is one of the lowest-friction opportunities because it improves an interaction customers already understand.

No one needs a tutorial explaining the search box.

### 4. Add conversational discovery where choosing is genuinely difficult

A guided AI assistant makes much more sense for:

- appliances;
- electronics;
- furniture;
- beauty;
- technical products;
- sporting equipment;
- complex B2B catalogues;

than it does for a store selling four varieties of tea towel.

AI is not improved by giving it unnecessary employment.

### 5. Measure customer outcomes, not AI activity

Don't celebrate because 4,000 customers interacted with an AI widget.

Measure whether it changed something useful:

- product-find rate;
- search exits;
- add-to-cart rate;
- conversion;
- revenue per session;
- average order value;
- returns;
- recommendation acceptance;
- time to suitable product.

Personalisation software should earn its place like any other commercial system.

## The real shift: from personalised marketing to personalised decision-making

The first generation of ecommerce personalisation changed **what businesses showed customers**.

The emerging generation is beginning to change **how customers decide**.

That is the more significant development.

Semantic search understands meaning.

Recommendation systems adapt using behaviour.

Conversational systems ask questions.

Generative AI explains differences.

Shopping agents increasingly conduct research outside the store itself.

All of those technologies are converging around the same commercial problem:

**There are too many products, too much information and too much work involved in deciding what to buy.**

The winning use of AI personalisation may therefore be much simpler than creating a mathematically perfect digital profile of every shopper.

It may be helping someone get from:

**“I vaguely know what I need”**

to:

**“That's the one.”**

with considerably less mucking around.

And for ecommerce, that is personalisation worth paying for.
